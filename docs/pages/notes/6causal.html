What is Causal Inference?

“Far better an approximate answer to the right question than an exact answer to the wrong question” — John Tukey

Causality is the broader study of causes, effects, and causal relationships, including philosophical questions about what causation means, how it operates, and what constitutes a causal explanation. Causal inference concerns estimating the effects of causes in contrast to causal structure discovery or causal model elicitation and causes of effects.

Associational vs causal (interventions)

Identification comes before estimation

Causal inference forces us to understand the differences in defining, identifying, and estimating a effect.

  1. Definition: What quantity are we interested in? For example, the average treatment effect, \(E[Y(1)-Y(0)]\).
  2. Identification: Under what assumptions can we express this causal quantity in terms of the observed-data distribution?
  3. Estimation: Given that expression, how can we estimate it from a finite sample?

These distinctions are there for simple associational estimands as well but they are rarely talked about outside construct validity and measruement errors. An estimator can have excellent statistical properties while estimating something that does not answer our causal question. More data, better prediction, or more sophisticated machine learning cannot resolve a failure of identification on their own.

Two especially important approaches to estimating causal effects are outcome modeling and weighting.

For a binary treatment, let

\[ m_a(x)=E[Y\mid A=a,X=x], \qquad e(x)=P(A=1\mid X=x). \]

Under consistency, conditional exchangeability, and positivity, the ATE can be identified through the outcome regression:

\[ ATE=E_X[m_1(X)-m_0(X)]. \]

This is the basis of standardization, or outcome-regression-based estimation. We estimate the expected outcome under each treatment and average over the target population’s covariate distribution.

Alternatively, the same effect can be identified through inverse probability weighting (IPW):

\[ ATE=E\left[ \frac{AY}{e(X)} - \frac{(1-A)Y}{1-e(X)} \right]. \]

Rather than modeling the outcome directly, IPW reweights observations according to their probability of receiving the treatment they actually received.

The important point is that these are different statistical representations of the same causal estimand, not different definitions of a causal effect.

Much of observational causal inference can be understood as trying to recover some of the inferential advantages of randomized experiments.

In a properly randomized trial, treatment assignment is independent of the potential outcomes by design. In observational studies, we generally do not have that luxury.

Methods such as matching, weighting, and standardization attempt to make comparisons between treatment groups meaningful under assumptions about the treatment assignment mechanism.

The target trial framework makes this connection particularly explicit: specify the hypothetical randomized experiment we would ideally conduct, then ask whether and how observational data can emulate it.

However, not all causal inference methods attempt to reproduce a conventional randomized trial. Instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, and difference-in-differences can exploit other sources of identifying variation, each with its own assumptions and often its own target estimand.

Randomization is a useful benchmark, but the larger principle is identifying what makes a comparison causally interpretable.

We also need to remember that a treatment effect belongs to a particular intervention, comparator, outcome, time horizon, and target population. Changing any of these can change the causal question itself.

Negative controls and falsification

Negative controls provide a useful gut check on causal analyses.

A negative control outcome is an outcome that the treatment is assumed not to affect. A negative control exposure is an exposure that is assumed not to affect the outcome of interest.

If an analysis suggests an effect where we believe none should exist, that raises questions about residual confounding, selection bias, measurement problems, or other failures of our assumptions.

For example, suppose we believe a treatment affects a particular health outcome but should have no effect on some unrelated outcome. Finding an association with that negative control outcome might suggest that the treatment and outcome groups differ in ways our analysis has not adequately addressed.

There are two important qualifications.

First, passing a negative-control check does not establish that our causal assumptions are correct. An analysis can fail to detect bias even when substantial bias remains.

Second, negative controls are not merely informal diagnostics. Under additional assumptions, they can help identify and adjust for otherwise unmeasured confounding, as in proximal causal inference.

The usefulness of a negative control therefore depends on why we believe the relevant causal effect is absent and what other relationships the control is expected to share with the variables of interest.

Causal uncertainty extends beyond sampling uncertainty to estimand uncertainty

Statistical inference typically quantifies uncertainty arising from the fact that we observe a finite sample rather than the entire population.

For example, a confidence interval may have valid frequentist coverage for an identified statistical functional under the assumed data-generating process and regularity conditions.

But statistical precision does not guarantee causal validity.

If exchangeability fails because of unmeasured confounding, a confidence interval can become arbitrarily narrow around a quantity that differs substantially from the causal effect we wanted.

Similarly, violations of positivity, inconsistently defined interventions, selection bias, and measurement error can all compromise causal conclusions in ways that ordinary standard errors do not capture.

This is why sensitivity analyses, falsification tests, alternative identification strategies, and partial identification are important complements to conventional statistical inference.

The distinction is simple: uncertainty about how well we estimated an identified quantity is not the same as uncertainty about whether that quantity has the causal interpretation we intended.

These ideas point to a common theme: causal inference requires us to separate what we want to know, what the data and assumptions allow us to know, and how precisely we can estimate it. Statistical methods address the last problem, but they cannot substitute for the first two.

DAGs

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) represents causal structure: the assumed causal relationships among variables. Each node represents a variable, and each arrow points from a cause (tail) to an effect (arrowhead). Directed means the arrows have a specified direction, while acyclic means that following the arrows cannot lead back to the same node. DAGs help us formulate causal questions, understand conditional independence, and determine what to adjust for (and what not to adjust for). In particular, they help us reason about confounding (common causes that create noncausal associations), colliders (common effects of two or more variables), and mediation (variables through which causal effects operate), without specifying a parametric statistical model.

Example: Parametric DAG: Structural equation modeling in psychology

A DAG specifies assumed causal relationships, but not the functional forms or distributions governing those relationships. A structural equation model (SEM) adds mathematical structure to the graph, allowing us to estimate relationships and examine whether their implications are compatible with observed data.

Consider a health psychologist studying how exercise (\(E\)), psychological hardiness (\(H\)), fitness (\(F\)), and stress (\(T\)) relate to illness (\(Y\)). Following the path model discussed by Kline (2023, Chapter 8), suppose exercise affects fitness, hardiness affects stress, and both fitness and stress affect illness:

\[ E\rightarrow F\rightarrow Y \leftarrow T\leftarrow H. \]

Exercise and hardiness are also allowed to be correlated, without specifying a causal direction between them. One possible linear structural equation model is

\[ \begin{aligned} E &= \varepsilon_E,\\ H &= \varepsilon_H,\\ F &= \alpha E+\varepsilon_F,\\ T &= \beta H+\varepsilon_T,\\ Y &= \gamma F+\delta T+\varepsilon_Y, \end{aligned} \]

where the errors have mean zero and are mutually independent except that \(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_E,\varepsilon_H)=\phi\) may be nonzero. Variables are centered for simplicity.

Here, \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\) represent direct path coefficients. Exercise has an indirect effect on illness of \(\alpha\gamma\) through fitness, while hardiness has an indirect effect of \(\beta\delta\) through stress, under the assumed causal model.

Why does covariance matter?

In traditional covariance-based SEM, the equations imply a particular covariance structure among the observed variables. For example,

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(F,T) =\alpha\beta\operatorname{Cov}(E,H) =\alpha\beta\phi. \]

Thus, even without a direct arrow between fitness and stress, they may be correlated because exercise and hardiness are correlated.

More importantly, the model implies

\[ F\perp\!\!\!\perp T\mid E,H, \]

because conditioning on exercise and hardiness blocks their connecting path. This is a testable implication of the assumed structure under the stated independent-error assumptions.

In matrix notation, the model-implied covariance matrix is

\[ \Sigma(\theta) =(I-B)^{-1}\Omega(I-B)^{-T}, \]

where \(B\) contains the structural path coefficients and \(\Omega\) contains the disturbance variances and allowed covariances.

We can compare the observed sample covariance matrix \(S\) with the model-implied covariance matrix \(\Sigma(\theta)\). A discrepancy suggests that some aspects of the proposed statistical model may be incorrect.

For five observed variables, the covariance matrix contains \(5(6)/2=15\) distinct entries. Our model has four path coefficients, five error variances, and one exogenous covariance, leaving \(15-10=5\) degrees of freedom for testing its covariance restrictions.

What happened in Kline’s example? Using data from 373 participants reported by Roth et al. (1989), Kline found a partial correlation of approximately \(-0.103\) between fitness and stress after controlling for exercise and hardiness (\(p=0.048\)). This conflicts with the model’s prediction of zero partial correlation and suggests a possible omitted relationship or other model misspecification.

This illustrates the connection between DAGs and SEM: the DAG tells us which conditional independences the assumed structure implies; the parametric SEM translates those assumptions into restrictions on the observed covariance structure. Neither good model fit nor a correctly specified covariance matrix, however, establishes that the assumed causal directions are correct.

Example: nonparametric example

However, DAGs do not encode all the assumptions required for causal inference. Their arrows do not specify the strength, functional form, or distribution of causal relationships. We must still consider internal and external validity, including construct validity, measurement error, effect modification (moderation), selection bias, and positivity. Some of these can be represented by extending a DAG but none of them is solved by increasing the sample size.

Confounding, Collision and Mediation

In a DAG conditioning determines which conditional independence are implied and which dependencies are possible. There are three basic substructures in DAG,

  • Chain (mediation): \(X\rightarrow M\rightarrow Y\). Conditioning on \(M\) blocks this path between \(X\) and \(Y\)
  • Fork (common cause, confounder if not blocked): \(X\leftarrow C\rightarrow Y\). Conditioning on \(C\) blocks this path between \(X\) and \(Y\)
  • Collider (common effect): \(X\rightarrow C\leftarrow Y\). This path is blocked by default, but conditioning on \(C\) or one of its descendants opens it
Remark: Collider Bias vs. Selection Bias

Collider bias can be identified by a DAG but selection bias occurs whenever the process determining which observations enter a sample distorts estimation. Selection bias can arise through collider bias, but the two are not identical, and neither is necessarily a subset of the other.

How it Works: Conditional Independence from DAGs

Two variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are d-separated by a conditioning set \(Z\) when every path connecting them is blocked

\[X\perp_d Y\]

Under the causal Markov assumption (each nodes noise is independt), d-separation implies conditional independence:

\[ X\perp_d Y\mid Z \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y\mid Z. \]

Once you list out all paths between two nodes, you apply the rules of d-separation to every single path to determine if the nodes are conditionally independent. For the two nodes to be conditionally independent given a set of conditioned variables \(Z\), every single path between them must be blocked. If even one path remains unblocked (open), the nodes are considered dependent.

Remark: Causal markov assumption

the causal markov assumption says that each nodes noise is independent

The converse need not hold without additional assumptions, such as faithfulness. In particular, an open path allows dependence but does not guarantee it.

What Do Adjustment and Randomization Do to a DAG?

  • Adjustment means conditioning on observed variables, corresponding to the “given” (\(\mid\)) in a conditional independence statement such as \(X\perp Y\mid Z\). Conditioning can block previously open paths or open previously blocked paths, changing the conditional independences implied by a DAG. When estimating the total causal effect of \(A\) on \(Y\), an appropriate adjustment set blocks noncausal paths without introducing new open noncausal paths or blocking causal pathways of interest.
    • Adjusting for a confounder can remove a noncausal association
    • Adjusting for a collider can introduce a noncausal association
    • Adjusting for a mediator can block part of the causal association we seek to estimate, without changing the underlying causal effect
  • Randomization changes the treatment assignment mechanism itself by uncoupling it from whatever it might’ve been. This is represented by removing the arrows entering a randomized variable in a DAG.
Remark: Adjustment is not model-dependent, but estimation can be

The graphical rules for adjustment do not depend on a statistical model. Whether conditioning on a variable opens or blocks a path follows from the assumed DAG, regardless of whether we use linear regression, logistic regression, machine learning, or another estimation method.

Markov Equivalence

Conditional independence relationships generally do not uniquely determine a DAG.

\[\begin{aligned} (X\rightarrow M\rightarrow Y) &\implies X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid M\\ (X\leftarrow M\rightarrow Y) &\implies X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid M \end{aligned}\]

Thus, even if we could perfectly recover all conditional independence relationships from observed data, we generally could only identify a Markov equivalence class: a collection of DAGs that imply exactly the same conditional independencies. From my limited understanding of causal structure learning, however, this is only the beginning of the difficulties. We rarely know which variables belong in the DAG in the first place. Important variables may be unmeasured, and in high-dimensional settings there can be an enormous number of possible relationships and conditioning sets to investigate. Moreover, conditional independences must be inferred from finite samples, introducing statistical uncertainty. No thanks! But more power to those who pursue such a tough problem…

Encoding More Complicated Situations in DAGs

DAGs can represent more than simple confounding, mediation, and colliders. By introducing additional nodes, we can describe features of real studies such as treatment noncompliance, exogenous shocks, and measurement error.

Example: Treatment Noncompliance

In a randomized trial, the treatment participants are assigned may differ from the treatment they actually receive. We can represent these as separate variables:

  • \(Z\): Randomized treatment assignment
  • \(A\): Treatment actually received
  • \(Y\): Outcome
  • \(U\): Unmeasured common causes of treatment received and outcome

A possible DAG contains

\[ Z\rightarrow A\rightarrow Y, \qquad A\leftarrow U\rightarrow Y. \]

Randomization removes common causes of \(Z\) and the other pre-treatment variables, but does not necessarily remove confounding between \(A\) and \(Y\). Under additional assumptions, including no direct effect of \(Z\) on \(Y\) except through \(A\), treatment assignment can serve as an instrumental variable for treatment received.

Example: Exogenous Shocks

An exogenous shock is an external event or intervention whose occurrence is not caused by the variables under study. Examples might include an unexpected policy change, natural disaster, or randomized intervention.

We can represent an ideal exogenous shock \(Z\) as a node with no incoming arrows, potentially affecting treatment \(A\) and outcome \(Y\):

\[ Z\rightarrow A\rightarrow Y. \]

However, exogeneity is an assumption, not something established by drawing a node without incoming arrows. Natural experiments require careful justification that the shock is sufficiently independent of relevant unmeasured causes. If we want to use \(Z\) as an instrumental variable, we also need assumptions such as exclusion.

Example: Measurement Error

DAGs can distinguish between a variable’s true value and what we actually measure. Let \(X\) denote the true variable, \(X^*\) its observed measurement, and \(\varepsilon\) a source of measurement error.

\[ X\rightarrow X^*\leftarrow\varepsilon. \]

For example, a patient’s true physical activity might affect their health outcome \(Y\), while researchers only observe self-reported activity \(X^*\).

\[ X\rightarrow Y, \qquad X\rightarrow X^*\leftarrow\varepsilon. \]

The graph makes clear that the measured variable is not necessarily the same as the underlying causal variable. Additional assumptions, such as \(X^*=X+\varepsilon\) with independent measurement error, specify how the measurements relate to the true values.

Potential Outcomes

Causal inference centers on interventions and the “what if” questions they raise, with causal estimands quantifying how outcomes would differ under alternative interventions.

A causal question compares what would happen to the target population under different interventions.

Definition: Potential Outcomes

Let \(A\in\{0,1\}\) denote treatment. For each individual, define \[ Y(1)=\text{outcome under treatment},\qquad Y(0)=\text{outcome under control}. \] The individual treatment effect (ITE) is \(\tau=Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\).

For the same individual at the same treatment occasion, we observe only one treatment condition. We therefore cannot observe both potential outcomes and directly calculate the individual treatment effect.This is the fundamental problem of causal inference. Under consistency (we observe the potential outcome under treatment \(Y=Y(A)\)), the observed outcome equals the potential outcome corresponding to the treatment received and the other potential outcome remains unobserved, \[ Y=Y(A)=AY(1)+(1-A)Y(0) \tag{1}\]

Estimands and estimators

An estimand is the quantity we want to learn; an estimator is a rule for calculating an estimate from observed data. Choosing an estimator does not, by itself, specify or identify a causal estimand.

Estimand Example estimator
Population mean, \(\mu=E[Y]\) Sample mean, \(\bar Y=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nY_i\)
Difference in observed-group means, \(E[Y\mid A=1]-E[Y\mid A=0]\) Difference in sample means, \(\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0\)
Average treatment effect, \(ATE=E[Y(1)-Y(0)]\) Difference in sample means in a randomized trial, under appropriate assumptions

Conversely, the same difference in sample means can estimate an observed-group contrast without identifying a causal effect. The research question must therefore specify a mathematical contrast—for example, a difference in mean outcomes, a risk ratio, or a difference in quantiles. We must also distinguish the population of interest from the population actually sampled. An ATE and an ATT can address different questions even when they use the same treatment, comparator, and outcome: \[ ATE=E[Y(1)-Y(0)], \qquad ATT=E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid A=1]. \] The ATE averages effects over the target population; the ATT averages them over those who received treatment.

ICH E9 (R1): Estimand Types

ICH E9(R1) complements PICOTS by considering how intercurrent events can affect the interpretation or existence of the outcome, such as treatment discontinuation, rescue medication, or death before assessment. Strategy Effect of interest
Treatment policy Outcomes regardless of the event’s occurrence, where those outcomes remain defined.

Composite Incorporate the event into the endpoint, such as counting rescue medication as treatment failure.
While on treatment Outcomes before the specified event occurs.
Principal stratum Effect within a subgroup defined by potential intercurrent events under specified treatment conditions.

These strategies define what effect we want, rather than merely how to analyze the data. Specifying that effect does not, by itself, establish its identification.

Causal effects/contrasts

Definition: Average Causal Effects

Assume \(E[|Y(a)|]<\infty\) for \(a=0,1\) and \(0<P(A=1)<1\). We can define causal estimands using expectations of the potential outcomes:

CATE \(\tau(x)=E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X=x]\) Average effect in the subgroup with covariates \(x\)

Remark: ITE vs CATE

We have to be careful to distinguish the individual effect \(\tau_i=Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\) and the conditional average treatment effect: \[ \tau(x)=E[\tau \mid X=x] = E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X=x]. \]

By iterated expectation, averaging the CATE over the target population’s covariate distribution gives the average treatment effect: \[ ATE=E[\tau] =E_X\!\left[E[\tau\mid X]\right] =E_X[\tau(X)]. \]

The CATE averages individual effects among people with covariates \(X=x\). Identifying \(\tau(x)\) does not generally identify those individual effects or the joint distribution of \((Y(0),Y(1))\).

Identification

After specifying a causal estimand, identification asks whether it is uniquely determined by the observed-data distribution under the stated assumptions. Equivalently, could two causal models satisfy those assumptions and generate exactly the same observed-data distribution while implying different values of the estimand? If so, the estimand is not identified.

Identification is distinct from estimating an identified quantity accurately in a finite sample. Increasing the sample size cannot resolve nonidentification under the same assumptions and observation scheme. Defending identification is therefore a central part of defending a causal conclusion.

This distinction, in my opinion, is one of the most important and most abused when empirical researchers interpret statistical tests. It is paramount to understand: a test of an observed-data contrast does not automatically test a causal effect. I return to this in “More Details on Identification.”

ATE: Standard Causal Identification Assumptions SUTVA, positivity, and exchangeability/ignorability

To identify the ATE as a functional of the observed-data distribution through standard covariate adjustment, we typically invoke SUTVA/consistency, positivity, and exchangeability (ignorability/no unmeasured confounding).

These assumptions require justification rather than being automatically established by the analysis. Clearly defined interventions and attention to interference support SUTVA; covariate-overlap and propensity-score diagnostics help assess positivity; and randomization or substantive causal knowledge supports exchangeability. DAGs make the latter assumptions explicit and help determine an appropriate adjustment set, but do not verify the assumptions they encode.

Assumption: SUTVA

Here, I use the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA) to include:

  • No interference: one individual’s outcome does not depend on other individuals’ treatment assignments.
  • No relevant hidden treatment versions: the treatment labels distinguish versions that could produce different potential outcomes, or versions grouped under a label yield the same potential outcome.
  • Consistency links the potential outcomes to the observed outcome: \[ A=a\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Y=Y(a). \] Equivalently, \[ Y=AY(1)+(1-A)Y(0)=Y(A). \tag{2}\]
Assumption: Positivity

Let \(e(x)=P(A=1\mid X=x)\) denote the propensity score. For identifying the ATE by covariate adjustment, a standard positivity condition is \[ 0<e(X)<1\qquad P_X\text{-almost surely}. \tag{3}\]

Both treatments must have positive probability at almost every covariate value in the target population. This is a population-level support condition, not a requirement that a finite sample contain treated and untreated individuals at every exact value of \(X\).

SUTVA and positivity alone do not make treatment groups comparable. Their observed outcomes may differ because of baseline prognosis or selection on treatment benefit. To justify the usual covariate-adjusted comparison, we need exchangeability!

Assumption: Conditional (Strong) Exchangeability

\[ \{Y(0),Y(1)\}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid X. \tag{4}\]

Given \(X\), treatment assignment carries no additional information about the potential outcomes. This is often called unconfoundedness, ignorability, or no unmeasured confounding relative to \(X\).

The Bayesian meaning of ignorability

The missing-data interpretation explains the word “ignorable.” Suppose, conditional on \(X\), the full-data model factors as \[ f_\theta(y_0,y_1\mid x)\,g_\psi(a\mid x), \] where exchangeability removes potential outcomes from the assignment model. Integrating the missing potential outcome yields \[ f_{\theta,a}(y\mid x)\,g_\psi(a\mid x). \] With distinct parameter spaces and independent priors \(\pi(\theta)\pi(\psi)\), the assignment factor does not affect the posterior for the outcome-model parameters \(\theta\). This is a likelihood-based meaning of ignorability, not permission to ignore \(X\) or assume away confounding. A prior also does not by itself establish identification. See Chapter 10 of Fan Li’s course notes.

Together, these assumptions connect the causal means to observed-data quantities.

Theorem: Identification by Standardization

Define \(m_a(x)=E[Y\mid A=a,X=x]\). Assume \(E[|Y(a)|]<\infty\) for \(a=0,1\). Under consistency, conditional mean exchangeability, and positivity, \[ \begin{aligned} E[Y(a)] &\overset{\text{iterated expectation}}{=} E_X\!\left[E[Y(a)\mid X]\right]\\ &\overset{\text{exchangeability and positivity}}{=} E_X\!\left[E[Y(a)\mid A=a,X]\right]\\ &\overset{\text{consistency}}{=} E_X[m_a(X)]. \end{aligned} \tag{5}\]

Thus, \[ \boxed{ATE=E_X[m_1(X)-m_0(X)].} \tag{6}\]

Positivity ensures that the observed-data conditional means are determined across the covariate distribution over which we average.

Theorem: Identification by Standardization

Personally, I find the role of positivity less obvious in the standardization argument. The observed-data distribution determines \(m_a\) only \(P_{X\mid A=a}\)-almost everywhere, while the integral averages over \(P_X\). Inverse probability weighting (IPW) makes this support requirement explicit through a change of measure.

Write \(e_a(x)=P(A=a\mid X=x)\). Positivity, \(e_a(X)>0\) \(P_X\)-almost surely, ensures absolute continuity: \[ P_X\ll P_{X\mid A=a}. \] Any covariate region absent from arm \(a\) must therefore also have zero probability in the target population. By Bayes’ rule, the Radon–Nikodym derivative is \[ \frac{dP_X}{dP_{X\mid A=a}}(x) =\frac{P(A=a)}{e_a(x)}. \] Starting from the standardization formula, \[ \begin{aligned} E[Y(a)] &=\int m_a(x)\,dP_X(x)\\ &=\int m_a(x)\, \frac{P(A=a)}{e_a(x)}\, dP_{X\mid A=a}(x)\\ &=E\!\left[\left.\frac{P(A=a)}{e_a(X)}Y\right|A=a\right]\\ &=E\!\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}(A=a)Y}{e_a(X)}\right]. \end{aligned} \] Consequently, with \(e(x)=P(A=1\mid X=x)\), \[ \boxed{ATE=E\!\left[\frac{AY}{e(X)}-\frac{(1-A)Y}{1-e(X)}\right].} \]

The weights transform the covariate distribution within arm \(a\) into the target distribution \(P_X\). Exchangeability and consistency supply the causal interpretation. If a target covariate region is absent from arm \(a\), neither weighting nor standardization can recover its counterfactual mean without additional assumptions: weighting lacks the required change of measure, while standardization has an undetermined integrand in that region.

ATT and ATU: changing the target population and causal identification assumptions

Different estimands can require different identification assumptions.

Under SUTVA, including consistency, the following mean-exchangeability and positivity conditions are sufficient. Write \(e(X)=P(A=1\mid X)\) and assume finite potential-outcome means and \(0<P(A=1)<1\).

Estimand Mean exchangeability required Positivity required Counterfactual means to recover
ATE \(E[Y(a)\mid A,X]=E[Y(a)\mid X]\) for \(a=0,1\) \(0<e(X)<1\), \(P_X\)-almost surely Untreated outcomes among those treated and treated outcomes among those untreated
ATT \(E[Y(0)\mid A,X]=E[Y(0)\mid X]\) \(e(X)<1\), \(P_{X\mid A=1}\)-almost surely Untreated outcomes among those treated
ATU \(E[Y(1)\mid A,X]=E[Y(1)\mid X]\) \(e(X)>0\), \(P_{X\mid A=0}\)-almost surely Treated outcomes among those untreated

For the ATT, consistency already identifies the treated potential-outcome mean among those treated; only their untreated mean must be recovered. For the ATU, the reverse applies.

Under the full set of assumptions used for the ATE, the same conditional contrast can be averaged over different target populations: \[ \begin{aligned} ATT&=E[m_1(X)-m_0(X)\mid A=1],\\ ATU&=E[m_1(X)-m_0(X)\mid A=0]. \end{aligned} \] These effects need not equal the ATE under conditional exchangeability: the covariate distributions being averaged over differ, and treatment effects may vary with \(X\).

Remark: Weaker Exchangeability Conditions

For identifying average effects, joint conditional independence of \(\{Y(0),Y(1)\}\) and \(A\) is stronger than necessary. It is sufficient to assume treatment-specific conditional independence, often called weak exchangeability, \[ Y(a)\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid X, \qquad a=0,1, \] or even the weaker conditional mean exchangeability restrictions, \[ E[Y(a)\mid A,X]=E[Y(a)\mid X], \qquad a=0,1, \] provided the relevant expectations exist. Together with consistency and positivity, either condition identifies the average effects.

Each estimand, however, requires identification of only one missing counterfactual mean. For the ATT, \(E[Y(1)\mid A=1]=E[Y\mid A=1]\) is already observed under consistency. We need only identify \(E[Y(0)\mid A=1]\), using the one-sided conditions in the table. No exchangeability assumption for \(Y(1)\) is required. With \(p=P(A=1)\), this gives \[ \boxed{ATT=\frac{1}{p}E\!\left[AY-(1-A)\frac{e(X)}{1-e(X)}Y\right].} \] The treated outcomes already represent the target group; the untreated outcomes receive odds weights \(e(X)/\{1-e(X)\}\).

Similarly, the ATU requires mean exchangeability only for \(Y(1)\) and treated support over the untreated covariate distribution. Then \[ \boxed{ATU=\frac{1}{1-p}E\!\left[A\frac{1-e(X)}{e(X)}Y-(1-A)Y\right].} \] In these one-sided weighting expressions, ratios need only be evaluated in the arm whose outcomes they weight; the corresponding summand is defined as zero outside that arm.

We can also relax the no-interference component of SUTVA to study spillover effects, where one person’s outcome depends on other people’s treatments. This is more subtle than simply dropping an assumption: we must expand the potential-outcome notation to represent those treatment assignments, define the effects of interest, and justify identification assumptions for them. Consistency still links the observed outcome to the potential outcome under the treatment configuration actually received.

Remark: What Does a Test of the Observed Mean Difference Test?

The question is then: are you testing what you think you are? Statistical significance can give a veneer of mathematical legitimacy to a causal conclusion that the research design does not support. A paper need not mention causal inference in its methods to make causal claims in its conclusions: saying that an intervention improved outcomes is a causal claim, even if the analysis is described only as a comparison of groups. Neither a small p-value nor a large sample closes that identification gap.

A valid test of \[ H_0:ATE_{\mathrm{obs}}=0 \] tests equality of the observed treatment-group means. Without further assumptions, it does not test \(H_0:ATE=0\). The decomposition above shows why: this null concerns the sum of the causal effect and the two selection terms, rather than the causal effect alone.

When \(ATE_{\mathrm{obs}}\neq 0\), a consistent test will reject with probability approaching one as the sample size grows, under suitable sampling conditions. It is correctly detecting an associational difference; interpreting that rejection as evidence of a treatment effect without establishing identification is the error.

Biostatistics: Randomized experiments

Random assignment supplies a known assignment mechanism. Under simple randomization with a common treatment probability, treatment is independent of pretreatment characteristics and potential outcomes. Blocked or unequal-probability designs require analysis that respects their assignment rules.

Fisher’s and Neyman’s modes of inference

The lecture distinguishes testing a sharp hypothesis from estimating an average effect. Both can use the randomization distribution; “exact versus asymptotic” is not the whole distinction.

Approach Typical target or null What randomization supplies
Fisher \(H_0:Y_i(1)=Y_i(0)\) for every \(i\) Under this sharp null, all missing outcomes are imputable, allowing a finite-sample randomization test
Neyman \(\tau_N=N^{-1}\sum_i\{Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\}\), or \(H_0:\tau_N=0\) Unbiased estimation and randomization variance; commonly large-sample confidence intervals and tests

A zero average allows positive and negative individual effects to cancel. It does not make the null sharp. A Fisher test of no individual effects is therefore not automatically a test of zero average effect under heterogeneity.

Result: Complete Randomization and the Difference in Means

For \(N\) fixed units, completely randomize exactly \(n_1\) to treatment and \(n_0=N-n_1\) to control. Then \[ \widehat\tau=\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0,\qquad E_A[\widehat\tau]=\tau_N, \] and \[ \operatorname{Var}_A(\widehat\tau) =\frac{S_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{S_0^2}{n_0}-\frac{S_\tau^2}{N}. \] Here \(S_a^2\) and \(S_\tau^2\) are finite-population variances with denominator \(N-1\). Because individual effects are unobserved, the usual variance estimator omits the last term: \[ \widehat V=\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_0^2}{n_0}. \] Its expectation is at least the true randomization variance. A normal-approximation interval requires additional large-sample conditions; conservative variance estimation alone is not an exact coverage theorem.

For a Fisher test, compute the statistic under each allowed assignment using the outcomes imputed under the sharp null. Compare the actual statistic to this distribution. Enumerating the assignments gives finite-sample type-I error control, potentially conservative because of discreteness. The permutations must follow the actual design.

Randomization supports inference for the trial participants. Generalizing to another population requires additional sampling or transportability arguments.

Adjustment for prognostic pretreatment covariates can improve precision and power; it is not needed to repair confounding created by simple randomization. Chance covariate imbalance can still occur in a realized trial.

One approach fits arm-specific regressions and evaluates both at the overall covariate distribution. For fixed-dimensional linear adjustment, fit \[ Y_i=\alpha+\beta A_i+\gamma^T(X_i-\bar X) +A_i\delta^T(X_i-\bar X)+\varepsilon_i. \] The treatment coefficient is the adjusted contrast at \(\bar X\). Under standard complete-randomization regularity conditions, this fully interacted estimator is consistent and asymptotically no less precise than the unadjusted estimator, even if the linear outcome model is misspecified. Use design-appropriate, heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. This is not a universal finite-sample improvement guarantee, especially with many covariates.

Target populations and overlap weighting

For a nonnegative function \(h\), define \[ \tau_h=\frac{E[h(X)\tau(X)]}{E[h(X)]},\qquad 0<E[h(X)]<\infty. \] The normalized weighted contrast uses \(w_1(X)=h(X)/e(X)\) and \(w_0(X)=h(X)/(1-e(X))\).

Overlap weighting targets \[ ATO=\frac{E[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}\tau(X)]}{E[e(X)\{1-e(X)\}]}. \] It emphasizes covariate profiles with plausible assignment to either arm. Its weights are bounded, but its estimand generally differs from ATE. With a finite, unpenalized logistic maximum-likelihood fit including an intercept, the score equations imply exact weighted mean balance for included covariate columns under overlap weights. This does not establish unmeasured balance.

Extreme inverse weights signal unstable comparisons. Trimming changes the represented population; truncating weights generally changes the estimating equation and can introduce bias. Report the target and examine balance and weight distributions. A useful weight diagnostic is \((\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2\), computed within each arm.

Augmented weighting and doubly robust estimation

Outcome regression predicts missing outcomes; weighting corrects representation. Augmented inverse probability weighting combines predictions with weighted residuals.

Estimator: AIPW for the ATE

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{ATE}_{\mathrm{AIPW}} =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\Bigg[&\widehat m_1(X_i)-\widehat m_0(X_i)\\ &+\frac{A_i}{\widehat e(X_i)}\{Y_i-\widehat m_1(X_i)\}\\ &-\frac{1-A_i}{1-\widehat e(X_i)}\{Y_i-\widehat m_0(X_i)\}\Bigg]. \end{aligned} \tag{10}\]

Under the causal assumptions and regularity conditions, consistency holds if either the propensity model is correct or both outcome mean models are correct. Double robustness concerns nuisance-model misspecification, not protection against unmeasured confounding or absent overlap.

Why double robustness works

For candidate functions \(q(X)\in(0,1)\) and \(r_a(X)\), consider the treated-mean contribution \[ U_1=r_1(X)+\frac{A}{q(X)}\{Y-r_1(X)\}. \] Conditioning on \(X\) gives \[ E[U_1\mid X] =r_1(X)+\frac{e(X)}{q(X)}\{m_1(X)-r_1(X)\}. \] Subtracting the desired \(m_1(X)\), \[ E[U_1\mid X]-m_1(X) =\frac{q(X)-e(X)}{q(X)}\{r_1(X)-m_1(X)\}. \] This is zero if either factor is zero. Repeating the calculation for the untreated mean yields the ATE bias identity \[ E[U_1-U_0]-ATE =E\left[(q-e)\left\{\frac{r_1-m_1}{q}+\frac{r_0-m_0}{1-q}\right\}\right]. \tag{11}\]

This identity is for fixed candidate functions; convergence of fitted models and control of empirical fluctuations are additional steps in proving estimator properties.

Inference with flexible nuisance estimates

Under both nuisance functions being consistently estimated, suitable overlap and moment assumptions, cross-fitting, and sufficiently small nuisance-error products, AIPW has influence function \[ \begin{aligned} \phi(O)=&\ m_1(X)-m_0(X)-ATE\\ &+\frac{A}{e(X)}\{Y-m_1(X)\} -\frac{1-A}{1-e(X)}\{Y-m_0(X)\}. \end{aligned} \] A typical sufficient rate requirement is \[ \|\widehat e-e\|_2 \left(\|\widehat m_1-m_1\|_2+\|\widehat m_0-m_0\|_2\right) =o_p(n^{-1/2}). \] Cross-fitting trains nuisance models outside each observation’s evaluation fold. Under these conditions, \[ \sqrt n(\widehat{ATE}-ATE)\rightsquigarrow N(0,E[\phi(O)^2]), \] with standard error estimated by the sample standard deviation of estimated influence values divided by \(\sqrt n\). Consistency under only one correct nuisance model does not automatically justify this particular standard-error formula; nuisance-estimation contributions may then need separate treatment.

Computer Science: DAGs, Structural Causal Models, and Causal Discovery

A prominent tradition in computer science and artificial intelligence emphasizes structural causal models, directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), and algorithms for reasoning about interventions.

I find DAGs useful, and working through the conditional independences they imply is a very useful exercise. Personally, though, I find the potential-outcomes formulation a cleaner starting point for defining treatment effects. It makes the target comparison explicit before introducing the graphical machinery.

These approaches are compatible. A structural causal model generates potential outcomes by replacing a variable’s structural equation with a specified intervention. The graph represents assumptions about the causal structure; potential outcomes express what would happen under particular interventions.

What does a DAG give us?

Consider the causal structure \[ X\longrightarrow A,\qquad X\longrightarrow Y,\qquad A\longrightarrow Y. \] Here, \(X\) is a common cause of treatment and outcome. Under the causal assumptions represented by this graph, adjusting for \(X\) blocks the backdoor path from \(A\) to \(Y\). With consistency and positivity, the resulting identification formula is the familiar standardization formula: \[ E[Y(a)] = \int E[Y\mid A=a,X=x]\,dP_X(x). \]

The graph therefore helps justify the adjustment set. It does not verify that the assumed causal structure is correct, and it does not tell us to adjust for every available variable. In particular, conditioning on a collider can create an association, while conditioning on a mediator can change the effect being targeted.

Do-calculus provides rules for transforming expressions involving interventions into identifiable expressions involving observed distributions, when the graph permits it. This goes beyond ordinary covariate adjustment. The notation can take some getting used to, but the underlying question is familiar: can the causal quantity be expressed using the data distribution under these assumptions?

Despite the term “Bayesian network,” these graphical representations do not require Bayesian estimation or priors. A probabilistic graph describes a factorization; a causal interpretation adds assumptions about what happens under intervention.

Causal discovery is a different task

Using a proposed DAG to identify an effect is different from attempting to learn the graph itself. Causal discovery uses features of the data, together with assumptions, to infer aspects of causal structure.

For example, the two graphs \[ A\longrightarrow Y \qquad\text{and}\qquad A\longleftarrow Y \] do not imply different conditional-independence restrictions when these are the only two variables. Observational association alone therefore cannot select the direction.

More generally, conditional-independence-based methods often identify a Markov equivalence class rather than a unique DAG. Distinguishing members may require interventions, temporal information, or additional functional assumptions. Common assumptions include the causal Markov condition and faithfulness; some methods also require no unmeasured common causes, while others allow latent confounding.

The word “discovery” should therefore be read carefully. The output remains conditional on the assumptions and on how well the data support the statistical decisions made by the algorithm.

There are much fuller introductions to DAGs than I will give here. Judea Pearl’s An Introduction to Causal Inference develops the structural-model perspective.

Psychometrics: Measurement and Construct Validity

Psychometrics approaches a question that is easy to skip once we have a dataset: what are we actually measuring?

Our scientific hypotheses may concern latent attributes such as happiness, sadness, anxiety, or mathematical ability. But the data contain questionnaire responses, test scores, or observed behaviors. Connecting those measurements to the attribute we care about is part of the scientific problem, not merely a preliminary formatting decision.

There are several competing views of validity. Personally, I find the causal account developed by Borsboom and colleagues especially compelling: an attribute must exist, and variation in that attribute must causally produce variation in the measurement outcomes. This directs attention toward the process that generates the score, rather than treating a collection of correlations as sufficient validation.

Example: An Effect on a Score Versus an Effect on a Construct

Suppose a randomized intervention is intended to reduce anxiety, and the outcome is a questionnaire score \(S\). Randomization may identify \[ E[S(1)-S(0)]. \] But the scientific claim may concern a latent anxiety attribute \(L\): \[ E[L(1)-L(0)]. \] These are different estimands.

For illustration, suppose the measurement process is \[ S(a)=\alpha+\lambda L(a)+\gamma a+\varepsilon(a), \qquad E[\varepsilon(a)]=0. \] Then \[ E[S(1)-S(0)] = \lambda E[L(1)-L(0)]+\gamma. \]

The term \(\gamma\) represents an effect of treatment on questionnaire scores that does not operate through anxiety—for example, changing how participants interpret the questions or how willing they are to report symptoms. Even a perfectly randomized trial cannot, by randomization alone, separate that reporting effect from an effect on anxiety.

Under this illustrative model, recovering the latent mean effect would require additional measurement assumptions, such as \(\gamma=0\) and a known nonzero scale factor \(\lambda\). The latent scale itself also needs a definition.

Factor analysis and item response theory provide tools for relating latent attributes to observed responses. Measurement-invariance analyses examine whether aspects of that relationship remain comparable across groups or occasions. However, good model fit, high reliability, or strong prediction does not by itself establish the intended causal measurement interpretation.

This is why I think measurement deserves much more attention in discussions of causal inference. We can identify an effect on the recorded outcome and still overstate what that effect tells us about the scientific construct.

For the causal perspective on validity, see Borsboom, Mellenbergh, and van Heerden’s The Concept of Validity.
