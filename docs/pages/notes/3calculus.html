Limits

Limits tell us what happens as one quantity approaches another,

Definition: Limits (Epsilon Delta)

Let \(f\) be a real-valued function defined on a domain containing all points sufficiently close to \(a\), except possibly at \(a\) itself. We say \[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \] if for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there exists a \(\delta > 0\) such that \[ 0 < \lvert x - a\rvert < \delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lvert f(x) - L\rvert < \varepsilon. \] In words, as \(x\) approaches \(a\) (but is not equal to \(a\)), \(f(x)\) can be made arbitrarily close to \(L\).

The \(\varepsilon–\delta\) definition is a pointwise definition, but it requires uniform control over all \(x\) sufficiently close to the specified point \(a\)

The \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) definition provides a rigorous characterization of limits, but applying it directly can be cumbersome. Fortunately, limits satisfy several useful algebraic properties, allowing us to calculate the complicated limits from simpler ones,

Theorem: Limit Laws and Properties

Let \(\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L\) and \(\lim_{x\to a}g(x)=M\) exist.

  1. Constants \[ \lim_{x\to a}c=c. \]

  2. Linearity: For constants \(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb R\), \[ \lim_{x\to a}[\alpha f(x)+\beta g(x)] =\alpha L+\beta M. \]

  3. Products \[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)g(x)=LM. \]

  4. Quotients: Provided \(M\neq0\), \[ \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{L}{M}. \]

  5. Powers and roots: For positive integers \(n\), provided the roots are defined near \(a\), \[ \lim_{x\to a}[f(x)]^n=L^n, \qquad \lim_{x\to a}\sqrt[n]{f(x)}=\sqrt[n]{L}. \]

  6. Continuous transformations: If \(h\) is continuous at \(L\), \[ \lim_{x\to a}h(f(x)) =h\left(\lim_{x\to a}f(x)\right) =h(L). \] the continuous transformation rule subsumes many of the other rules

Example: Limit Properties

We verify each basic limit law with a concrete example,

  1. Constant rule. \[ \lim_{x\to 5} 7 = 7. \]

  2. Sum/Difference. Let \(f(x)=2x\), \(g(x)=3\). Then \(\lim_{x\to2}f(x)=4\), \(\lim_{x\to2}g(x)=3\), and \[ \lim_{x\to2}[f(x)+g(x)] = \lim_{x\to2}(2x+3) = 2\cdot2+3 = 7 = 4+3. \]

  3. Product. With the same \(f,g\) at \(x\to2\), \[ \lim_{x\to2}[f(x)\,g(x)] = \lim_{x\to2}(2x\cdot3) = (2\cdot2)\cdot3 = 12 = 4\cdot3. \]

  4. Quotient. Let \(f(x)=x^2-1\), \(g(x)=x-1\). Then as \(x\to1\), \(\lim f(x)=0\), \(\lim g(x)=0\), but for \(x\neq1\) \[ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = x+1. \] Hence \[ \lim_{x\to1}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to1}(x+1) = 2 = \frac{0}{0}\text{--resolved by cancellation.} \]

  5. Power and root. Let \(f(x)=x+3\). At \(x\to1\), \(\lim f(x)=4\). Then \[ \lim_{x\to1}[f(x)]^3 = 4^3 = 64, \qquad \lim_{x\to1}\sqrt{f(x)} = \sqrt{4} = 2. \]

  6. Composition. Let \(f(x)=\sin x\), \(h(u)=u^2\). As \(x\to0\), \(\lim f(x)=0\), and \(h\) is continuous at \(0\), so \[ \lim_{x\to0}h\bigl(f(x)\bigr) = \lim_{x\to0}[\sin x]^2 = [\lim_{x\to0}\sin x]^2 = 0^2 = 0. \]

Limits naturally allow us to consider approaching \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\) and the above laws still apply. There are also several particularly useful limit consequences,

Theorem: Squeeze theorem

If \(g(x)\le f(x)\le h(x)\) near \(a\) and \[ \lim_{x\to a}g(x)=\lim_{x\to a}h(x)=L \ \implies \ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L \]

Example: Squeeze Theorem

Suppose we want to find the following limit \[\lim_{x\to 0} x^2 \sin\!\bigl(\tfrac1x\bigr) = 0\] Recall that for all real \(t\), \[-1 \;\le\; \sin(t) \;\le\; 1\] Multiplying by \(x^2\ge0\) gives \[-\,x^2 \;\le\; x^2\sin\!\bigl(\tfrac1x\bigr)\;\le\; x^2\] But \[\lim_{x\to0}(-x^2)=0, \quad \lim_{x\to0}x^2=0\] By the Squeeze Theorem, it follows that \[\lim_{x\to0}x^2\sin\!\bigl(\tfrac1x\bigr)=0\]

We can visualize the squeeze theorem as well,

Theorem: L’Hopital’s Rule

If \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable near \(a\) (except possibly at \(a\)) and
\[\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=\lim_{x\to a}g(x)=0 \quad\text{or}\quad \pm\infty,\] and \(g'(x)\neq0\) near \(a\), then \[\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \;=\; \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)},\] provided the right-hand limit exists (or is \(\pm\infty\)).

Example: L’Hopital’s Rule

\[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x} \;{=}\; \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\cos x}{1} =1\]

How Limits are Used in Statistics

  1. Limits provide the rigorous foundation for familiar analytic concepts such as derivatives and integration.
  2. Limits allow us to study the behavior of statistical procedures as the amount of data grows. Statements about consistency, convergence in probability, convergence in distribution, laws of large numbers, central limit theorems, and asymptotic approximations are all statements about limits as the sample size \(n\to\infty\), of course we will never reach \(\infty\) though

Differentiation

Functions change at different rates, and one way to measure this change is through the slope of a secant line, which measures the average rate of change between two points on a graph.

To see this, take \(c>0\) and consider two points \(P=(a-c,f(a-c))\) and \(Q=(a+c,f(a+c))\) on the graph of a function \(f\). These points lie on opposite sides of \(a\), each a horizontal distance \(c\) away. The slope of the secant line joining them is rise over run, or

\[ \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} =\frac{f(a+c)-f(a-c)}{(a+c)-(a-c)} =\frac{f(a+c)-f(a-c)}{2c}. \]

As \(c\to 0\), the two points approach \((a,f(a))\), provided \(f\) is continuous at \(a\). If \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\), the slopes approaching from either side must converge to the same value. Consequently, the symmetric secant slopes also approach the slope of the tangent line at \(a\), called the instantaneous rate of change, or derivative:

\[ f'(a)=\lim_{c\to 0^+}\frac{f(a+c)-f(a-c)}{2c}. \]

Because of the constant \(2\) multiplied by \(c\) we usually take \(h = 2c \to 0\) equivalently

Example: The absolute value function is not differentiable at zero

The existence of the limit of \(\frac{f(a+c)-f(a-c)}{2c}\) does not necessarily imply differentiability. Consider \(f(x)=|x|\) at \(a=0\). For every \(c>0\), \[ \frac{f(c)-f(-c)}{2c} =\frac{|c|-|-c|}{2c}=0. \]

However, the slopes approaching zero from the left and right are different: \[ \lim_{h\to0^-}\frac{|h|-|0|}{h}=-1, \qquad \lim_{h\to0^+}\frac{|h|-|0|}{h}=1. \]

Since these one-sided limits disagree, \(f'(0)\) does not exist, despite the quotient always equaling zero.

Thus, differentiability guarantees convergence of \(\frac{f(a+c)-f(a-c)}{2c}\), but the converse is not necessarily true.

Definition: Derivative

Let \(f\) be a function defined in a neighborhood of \(a\in\mathbb{R}\). The derivative of \(f\) at \(a\) is the limit of the symmetric difference quotient, provided it exists,

\[f'(a) := \lim_{c\to 0} \frac{f(a+c)-f(a-c)}{2c} = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\]

If this limit exists (and is finite), we say \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\), and \(f'(a)\) gives the instantaneous rate of change of \(f\) with respect to \(x\) at \(x=a\).

Example: Common Derivatives

We compute the derivatives at \(a=1\) of \(f(x) = x,f(x) = x^2, f(x) = e^{x}\) by taking limits.

  1. \(\displaystyle f(x)=x\). \[f'(1) =\lim_{c\to0}\frac{(1+c)-(1-c)}{2c} =\lim_{c\to0}\frac{2c}{2c} =1\]

  2. \(\displaystyle f(x)=x^2\). \[\begin{aligned}f'(1) &=\lim_{c\to0}\frac{(1+c)^2-(1-c)^2}{2c}\\ &=\lim_{c\to0}\frac{(1+2c+c^2)-(1-2c+c^2)}{2c}\\ &=\lim_{c\to0}\frac{4c}{2c} =2\end{aligned}\]

  3. \(\displaystyle f(x)=e^x\). \[f'(1) =\lim_{c\to0}\frac{e^{1+c}-e^{1-c}}{2c} =\lim_{c\to0}\frac{e\,(e^c-e^{-c})}{2c} =e\;\lim_{c\to0}\frac{e^c-e^{-c}}{2c} =e\cdot1 =e\] since \(\lim_{c\to0}(e^c-e^{-c})/(2c)=1\)

Remark: Differentiation operator

We generally write \(f'(a)\) for the derivative of \(f\) at \(a\), while \(\frac{d}{dx}\) denotes the differentiation operator with respect to \(x\). This operator takes a differentiable function \(f\) and returns another function, its derivative:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}f(x)=f'(x). \]

Evaluating this derivative at \(x=a\) gives the instantaneous rate of change \(f'(a)\).

Theorem: Differentiation Rules

Differentiation is a linear operator. For differentiable functions \(f,g\) and constants \(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb R\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}[\alpha f(x)+\beta g(x)] =\alpha f'(x)+\beta g'(x). \]

Thus, the derivative of a linear combination is the same linear combination of the derivatives. However, differentiation is more than just a linear operator and it also satisfies several other important rules that are not direct consequences of linearity:

  1. Power rule: For any real constant \(n\), wherever the expression is differentiable, \[ \frac{d}{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1}. \]

  2. Product rule: If \(h(x)=f(x)g(x)\), then \[ h'(x)=\frac{d}{dx}[f(x)g(x)] =f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x). \]

  3. Quotient rule: If \(h(x)=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\), where \(g(x)\neq 0\), then \[ h'(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] =\frac{f'(x)g(x)-f(x)g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2}. \]

  4. Chain rule: If \(h(x)=f(g(x))\), then \[ h'(x)=\frac{d}{dx}[f(g(x))] =f'(g(x))g'(x). \]

Univariate Optimization

Differentiation helps us optimize functions: find where a function takes its largest (maximum) or smallest (minimum) value. These extrema may be local, relative to nearby points, or global, relative to the entire domain. A useful starting point is to distinguish stationary points, where \(f'(c)=0\), from roots, where \(f(c)=0\). They are not generally the same.

Theorem: Roots and Stationary Points

Let \(f\) be differentiable on an open interval containing \(c\).

  • A root (or zero) of \(f\) is a point where \(f(c)=0\), while a stationary point is a point where \(f'(c)=0\).

  • If \(f\) has a local maximum or minimum at the interior point \(c\), then \(f'(c)=0\). This holds whether or not \(f(c)=0\).

Thus, differentiation can help us locate possible extrema, and sometimes a stationary point is also a root. But not every root is stationary, and not every stationary point is a root.

Example: Finding a root using derivatives

Consider \[ f(x)=(x-1)^2 \] on the interval \([0,2]\). Differentiating gives \[ f'(x)=2(x-1). \]

Solving \[ f'(x)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2(x-1)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=1. \]

Checking the original function, \[ f(1)=(1-1)^2=0. \]

Thus, \(x=1\) is both a root and a stationary point. This works because the root has multiplicity 2, so the graph touches the horizontal axis at a local minimum rather than crossing it.

In contrast, \(g(x)=x-1\) also has a root at \(x=1\), but \(g'(1)=1\neq0\). Differentiation alone would not detect this root.

Differentiating a function multiple times tells us how its rate of change is itself changing. The first derivative \(f'(x)\) describes the instantaneous rate of change of \(f\), while the second derivative \(f''(x)\) describes the instantaneous rate of change of \(f'\). In particular, the sign of the second derivative tells us about the function’s concavity or convexity:

  • If \(f''(x)>0\), the slope is increasing and the function is locally convex (curving upward).
  • If \(f''(x)<0\), the slope is decreasing and the function is locally concave (curving downward).

Together, the first and second derivatives help us locate and classify extrema. In particular, the shape of a function can tell us when a stationary point is not just a local extremum, but a global one.

Theorem: Global Extrema via Derivatives

Let \(f:I\to\mathbb{R}\) be twice continuously differentiable on an open interval \(I\), and suppose \(x_0\in I\) satisfies \(f'(x_0)=0\).

  1. Global minimum: If \(f''(x)>0\) for all \(x\in I\), then \(f\) is strictly convex on \(I\), and \(x_0\) is the unique global minimizer: \[ f(x_0)=\min_{x\in I}f(x). \]

  2. Global maximum: If \(f''(x)<0\) for all \(x\in I\), then \(f\) is strictly concave on \(I\), and \(x_0\) is the unique global maximizer: \[ f(x_0)=\max_{x\in I}f(x). \]

Thus, if the second derivative is strictly positive or negative throughout the interval, any stationary point is automatically the unique global extremum of the corresponding type.

The important idea is that the shape of the function guarantees the uniqueness of the stationary point and global extremum, provided a stationary point exists. In higher dimensions, derivatives generalize to gradients and Hessian matrices, and convexity can be studied through positive definiteness. The mathematics gets more involved, but the underlying principle is the same.

Example: Global extrema via derivatives

Consider two functions defined on \(\mathbb{R}\).

  1. Global minimum: Let \(f(x)=x^2-1\). Then \[ f'(x)=2x,\qquad f'(0)=0, \qquad f''(x)=2>0. \] Hence \(f\) is strictly convex on \(\mathbb{R}\), and \(x_0=0\) is the unique global minimizer: \[ f(0)=-1=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f(x). \]

  2. Global maximum: Let \(g(x)=1-x^2\). Then \[ g'(x)=-2x,\qquad g'(0)=0, \qquad g''(x)=-2<0. \] Hence \(g\) is strictly concave on \(\mathbb{R}\), and \(x_0=0\) is the unique global maximizer: \[ g(0)=1=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}g(x). \]

Definition: Smooth functions

A function is called smooth if it has continuous derivatives of every order, also called infinitely differentiable and denoted \(C^\infty\).

Intuitively, smooth functions have no sharp corners or kinks, and we can repeatedly differentiate them without running into points where a derivative fails to exist. A function that is not smooth is called nonsmooth.

More generally, \(C^k\) denotes the class of functions with continuous derivatives up to order \(k\).

Example: Smooth and nonsmooth functions

Consider two familiar functions:

  1. Smooth: The function \(f(x)=x^2\) has derivatives \[ f'(x)=2x,\qquad f''(x)=2,\qquad f^{(k)}(x)=0\quad(k\ge3). \] All derivatives exist and are continuous everywhere, so \(f\in C^\infty(\mathbb{R})\).

  2. Nonsmooth: The function \(g(x)=|x|\) has derivative \[ g'(x)= \begin{cases} -1, & x<0,\\ 1, & x>0. \end{cases} \] At \(x=0\), the left and right derivatives disagree, producing a sharp corner. Thus, \(g\) is continuous everywhere but not differentiable at zero, and therefore not smooth.

Notice that smoothness is stronger than differentiability. A function can have a well-defined first derivative everywhere without having continuous derivatives of every order.

Existence and Location of Global Extrema

When optimizing a function on a closed interval \([a,b]\), we need to consider both the interior and the endpoints. But before finding the extrema, how do we know they even exist? This question leads us to compactness.

Definition: Compactness

In \(\mathbb R\), a set \(K\) is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded, a result known as the Heine–Borel theorem. In particular, every closed interval \([a,b]\) is compact.

Another way to characterize compactness is through sequences: every sequence of elements in \(K\) has a subsequence converging to a point in \(K\). This is called sequential compactness.

More generally, compactness is defined by requiring that every open cover has a finite subcover. These definitions are equivalent in metric spaces, including \(\mathbb R\).

Theorem: Extreme Value Theorem

Every continuous real-valued function on a nonempty compact set attains both a global minimum and a global maximum.

Example: When Extrema Are Not Attained

Consider \(f(x)=x\) on the open interval \((0,1)\). We have

\[ \inf_{x\in(0,1)}f(x)=0, \qquad \sup_{x\in(0,1)}f(x)=1, \]

but neither value is attained. The interval is not compact, and the function has no global minimum or maximum. Compactness guarantees that continuous functions attain their extrema, although it is not necessary in every case.

Once we know the extrema exist, we can find them by checking the endpoints and any interior stationary points, provided the function is differentiable in the interior.

Theorem: Global Extrema on a Closed Interval

Let \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R\) be continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\). Define the set of candidates

\[ \mathcal C=\{c\in(a,b):f'(c)=0\}\cup\{a,b\}. \]

Then

\[ \min_{x\in[a,b]}f(x)=\min_{x\in\mathcal C}f(x), \qquad \max_{x\in[a,b]}f(x)=\max_{x\in\mathcal C}f(x). \]

Remark: We Must Check the Endpoints

The usual “derivative equals zero” condition only applies at interior extrema, where we can look infinitesimally to the left and right. At a boundary, we don’t have that two-sided wiggle room, so the endpoints must be checked separately.

Example: Finding Global Extrema on a Closed Interval

Let

\[ f(x)=x^2-2x, \qquad [a,b]=[-1,3]. \]

  1. Find the interior stationary points:

    \[ f'(x)=2x-2, \qquad f'(x)=0\Longrightarrow x=1. \]

  2. Evaluate at the endpoints and at \(x=1\):

    \[ f(-1)=3,\qquad f(1)=-1,\qquad f(3)=3. \]

  3. Compare:

    \[ \min_{x\in[-1,3]}f(x)=f(1)=-1, \]

    \[ \max_{x\in[-1,3]}f(x)=f(-1)=f(3)=3. \]

Thus, the global minimum occurs at \(x=1\), while the global maxima occur at both endpoints, \(x=-1\) and \(x=3\).

Example: Global Extrema Without Interior Stationary Points

Consider the function

\[ f(x)=x \quad\text{on the interval}\quad [0,1]. \]

Since

\[ f'(x)=1 \quad\text{for all }x\in(0,1), \]

there are no stationary points in the interior. However, evaluating at the endpoints gives

\[ f(0)=0,\qquad f(1)=1. \]

Hence,

\[ \min_{x\in[0,1]}f(x)=f(0)=0, \qquad \max_{x\in[0,1]}f(x)=f(1)=1. \]

Both global extrema occur at the boundaries, despite there being no interior stationary points.

Non-Linear Optimization and Iteration: Newton’s (Raphson) Method

If our derivative is a linear equation \[f'(x)=m x + b = 0\] then we can arrive at the sole solution \(x^* = -\tfrac{b}{m}\) from solving for the critical point directly.

However, if \(f'\) is a smooth but nonlinear function, then Newton’s method helps us find roots iteratively starting at some reasonable guess \(x_1\) and moving towards the solution at each iteration. Instead of thinking of \(f'\) we just use \(g\) more generally,

Newton’s method updates \[x_{n+1} \;=\; x_n \;-\;\frac{g(x_n)}{g'(x_n)}\] by solving for the \(x\) where the tangent line crosses the x-axis, \[L_n(x) \;=\; g(x_n) +g'(x_n)\,(x - x_n) = 0\]

Proof

Let \(g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) be differentiable and fix an iterate \(x_n\). The tangent line to the graph of \(y=g(x)\) at \(x_n\) is the first-order taylor approximation of \(g\) about \(x_n\) \[L_n(x) = g(x_n) + g'(x_n)\,(x - x_n)\]

We can also arrive at \(L_n(x)\) by noting that \[g'(x_n)= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{g(x_n + h) - g(x_n)}{h}\] we need the line with this slope to pass through \((x_n,g(x_n))\) and writing this in point-slope form \[y-g(x_n) = g'(x_n)(x-x_n)\implies y= g(x_n)+ g'(x_n)(x-x_n) = L_n(x)\] Regardless how we justify \(L_n(x)\), we define the next iterate \(x_{n+1}\) to be the point where this line crosses the \(x\)-axis, i.e. we solve \[0 = L_n(x_{n+1}) = g(x_n) + g'(x_n)\,\bigl(x_{n+1}-x_n\bigr)\] Rearranging gives \[x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{g(x_n)}{g'(x_n)}\]

The idea is that where the tangent line crosses the x-axis is not too far from where the actual function crosses. In fact under rather mild conditions that \(g\) is twice continuously differentiable in an open interval about the true root \(\beta\), \(g(\beta) = 0\) and $g’() $, so we don’t have a horizontal tangent line at the root

Trying to find roots of nonlinear functions is not always straightforward and newton’s method gives us an approximation to these roots given an initial guess. This technique becomes more relevant in generalized linear models around the second year coursework rather than in the first year but can occasionally be useful in simpler contexts.

Example

Integration and Antiderivatives

Integration tells us how much of a function “accumulates” in a given area between the \(x\) axis and the function,

The antiderivative (or indefinite integral) is the functional counterpart of this accumulation process, that is the integral is the actual area under the curve for a specified domain and the antiderivative/indefinite integral is the more general concept,

Definition (Indefinite Integral, Antiderivative)

Define \(A\) such that, \[ A(x) = \int_{a}^{x} f(t),\mathrm{d}t, \] which by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus satisfies, \[ A'(x) = f(x) \quad\text{and}\quad A(a)=0. \] More generally, any function \(F\) whose derivative is \(f\) is called an antiderivative of \(f\), and we write \[ \int f(x),\mathrm{d}x = F(x) + C, \] where \(C\) is an arbitrary constant. In this sense, integration “undoes” differentiation.

Integrals have several useful properties,

Theorem (Integration Rules)

  • Definite integral: for a continuous \(f\) on \([a,b]\), \[ \int_a^b f(x) \ dx = F(b)-F(a), \] where \(F'!=f\)

  • Antiderivative: \(F\) is any function with \(F'(x)=f(x)\).

  • Substitution rule: if \(u = g(x)\) is differentiable, \[\int f(g(x)),g'(x) \ dx = \int f(u)du\]

  • Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: \[ \frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)dt = f(x), \quad \int_a^b f'(x) \ dx = f(b)-f(a). \]

Example

  1. Definite integral. Compute \[ \int_{0}^{2} 3x^2 \ dx = \bigl[x^3\bigr]_{0}^{2} = 8 \]

  2. Antiderivative. For \(f(x)=\sin x\), an antiderivative is \[ F(x) = -\cos x + C, \quad F'(x)=\sin x \]

  3. Substitution rule. Evaluate \[ \int 2x,e^{x^2} \ dx \] Let \(u = x^2\), so \(du = 2x \ dx\). Then \[ \int 2x,e^{x^2} \ dx = \int e^udu = e^u + C = e^{x^2} + C \]

  4. Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

    1. Differentiation under the integral sign: \[ \frac{d}{dx}\int_{1}^{x} t^3dt = x^3 \]
    2. Definite integral of a derivative: \[ \int_{1}^{2} \frac{d}{dx}(x^3) \ dx = \bigl[x^3\bigr]_{1}^{2} = 8 - 1 = 7 \]
Remark

Unlike differentiation, integration is a global (non-local) operation and an inverse problem that need not admit an elementary closed-form. That is we may encounter integrals that are intractable analytically and instead must rely on numerical techniques to approximate them

Function Approximation, Taylor’s Theorem, and Little-\(o\)/Big-\(O\) Notation

A function may be difficult to work with directly, but near a chosen point we can often replace it by a simpler linear or quadratic expression. In statistics, this lets us approximate a transformed estimator or solve an estimating equation. We begin with an exact identity and then identify the approximation error.

Theorem (Exact Taylor representation)

Suppose \(f\) is continuously differentiable on an interval containing \(a\) and \(x\). The fundamental theorem of calculus gives

\[ f(x)-f(a)=\int_a^x f'(t)\,dt. \]

For \(x\ne a\), dividing by \(x-a\),

\[ \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a} = \frac{1}{x-a}\int_a^x f'(t)\,dt. \]

The left-hand side is the secant slope between the points \((a,f(a))\) and \((x,f(x))\), while the right-hand side is the average of the tangent slopes \(f'(t)\) over the interval from \(a\) to \(x\). Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{secant slope} = \text{average tangent slope over the interval}. } \]

Equivalently, using the change of variables \(t=a+s(x-a)\) for \(s\in[0,1],\)

\[ f(x) = f(a) + (x-a) \left[ \frac{1}{x-a}\int_a^x f'(t)\,dt \right]=f(a) + (x-a) \int_0^1 f'\bigl(a+s(x-a)\bigr)\,ds \]

This is the exact Taylor representation of \(f(x)\) about \(x=a\), asserting the exact change in \(f\) is the distance \(x-a\) multiplied by the average derivative encountered while moving from \(a\) to \(x\).

Theorem (First-order Taylor approximation)

The first-order Taylor approximation is found by adding and subtracting \(f'(a)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &= f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)-f'(a)(x-a) + (x-a) \int_0^1 \left[ f'\bigl(a+s(x-a)\bigr) \right]ds. \\ &=f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)+ (x-a) \int_0^1 \left[ f'\bigl(a+s(x-a)\bigr)-f'(a) \right]ds. \end{aligned} \]

Hence

\[ f(x) = f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+R(x,a), \]

where the exact remainder is

\[ R(x,a) = (x-a) \int_0^1 \left[ f'\bigl(a+s(x-a)\bigr)-f'(a) \right]ds. \]

If \(f'\) is continuous at \(a\), then as \(x\to a\),

\[ f'\bigl(a+s(x-a)\bigr)\to f'(a) \]

uniformly over \(s\in[0,1]\). Therefore,

\[ \frac{R(x,a)}{|x-a|} \to 0. \]

This is written

\[ R(x,a)=o(|x-a|), \]

so that

\[ \boxed{ f(x) = f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+o(|x-a|). } \]

Thus the first-order Taylor approximation means that the error is negligible relative to the size of \(x-a\).

Theorem (Higher-order Taylor expansion)

More generally, if \(f\) has \(k\) continuous derivatives near \(a\), Taylor’s theorem gives

\[ f(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2 +\cdots+ \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k + R_k(x,a). \]

As \(x\to a\),

\[ R_k(x,a)=o(|x-a|^k). \]

For example,

\[ f(x) = f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+O((x-a)^2) \]

when the second derivative is locally bounded.

To describe how these errors shrink as the expansion point is approached, we use order notation.

Big-\(O\) notation

Definition (Big-O notation)

For deterministic functions \(r(x)\) and \(g(x)>0\), we write

\[ r(x)=O(g(x)) \qquad\text{as }x\to a \]

if there exists a finite constant \(C\) such that, for all \(x\) sufficiently close to \(a\),

\[ |r(x)|\le C g(x). \]

Thus \(O(g(x))\) means that \(r(x)\) is at most of the same order as \(g(x)\).

Equivalently,

\[ \frac{r(x)}{g(x)} \]

remains bounded as \(x\to a\).

Example (A quadratic term is bounded by a linear rate)

For example,

\[ (x-a)^2=O(|x-a|) \]

as \(x\to a\), because for \(x\) sufficiently close to \(a\),

\[ \frac{(x-a)^2}{|x-a|} = |x-a| \]

is bounded.

Little-\(o\) notation

Definition (Little-o notation)

We write

\[ r(x)=o(g(x)) \qquad\text{as }x\to a \]

if

\[ \frac{r(x)}{g(x)}\to0. \]

Thus \(o(g(x))\) means that \(r(x)\) is asymptotically negligible relative to \(g(x)\).

Example (Negligible versus bounded error)

For example,

\[ (x-a)^2=o(|x-a|) \]

because

\[ \frac{(x-a)^2}{|x-a|} = |x-a| \to0. \]

The distinction is therefore

\[ r(x)=O(g(x)) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{r(x)}{g(x)} \text{ is bounded}, \]

whereas

\[ r(x)=o(g(x)) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{r(x)}{g(x)} \to0. \]

In particular,

\[ o(g(x))\subset O(g(x)), \]

in the sense that every term that is little-\(o\) of \(g(x)\) is also big-\(O\) of \(g(x)\), but not conversely.

Taylor expansions are useful precisely because they separate a function into a leading term and a remainder whose order can be controlled. For example,

\[ f(x)-f(a) = f'(a)(x-a) + o(|x-a|) \]

says that, when \(f'(a)\ne0\), the linear term gives the leading change in \(f\). If \(f'(a)=0\), a higher-order term may determine the leading behavior. Dividing through by \(x-a\),

\[ \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a} = f'(a)+o(1), \]

which recovers the statement that the secant slope converges to the tangent slope.

The single-variable setup applies when we vary one scalar input or parameter. Many statistical problems involve several parameters, even when each response is univariate. When an observation has \(p\) characteristics/covariates, we arrange them into a vector

\[ \mathbf X_i =\begin{bmatrix} X_{i1}, X_{i2}, \cdots, X_{ip} \end{bmatrix}^\top \in\mathbb R^p. \]

In parametric statistics, the corresponding model parameters are arranged into the coefficient vector

\[ \boldsymbol\beta =\begin{bmatrix} \beta_1, \beta_2, \cdots, \beta_p \end{bmatrix}^\top \in\mathbb R^p. \]

Generally, parametric statistical estimation involves a scalar-valued criterion function indexed by a finite-dimensional parameter vector,

\[ Q:\mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R, \]

where

\[ Q(\boldsymbol\beta) =Q(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_p), \qquad \boldsymbol\beta\in\mathbb R^p. \]

The criterion function \(Q\) may represent a log-likelihood, negative log-likelihood, loss function, posterior log density, or another estimating criterion. Although \(Q(\boldsymbol\beta)\) returns a single scalar value, it may depend jointly on all components of \(\boldsymbol\beta\) and need not separate into coordinate-specific terms.

Statistical estimation then proceeds by optimizing \(Q(\boldsymbol\beta)\) with respect to the parameter vector \(\boldsymbol\beta\). The derivative becomes a vector because there is now one direction of change for each coordinate.

Definition (Gradient)

The gradient of \(Q\) is the vector of componentwise partial derivatives,

\[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}Q(\boldsymbol\beta) =\begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle \frac{\partial Q(\boldsymbol\beta)}{\partial\beta_1}\\ \displaystyle \frac{\partial Q(\boldsymbol\beta)}{\partial\beta_2}\\ \vdots\\ \displaystyle \frac{\partial Q(\boldsymbol\beta)}{\partial\beta_p} \end{bmatrix}. \]

Each component gives the rate of change of \(Q\) as one parameter \(\beta_j\) varies while the remaining parameters are held fixed.

Example: linear functions

Consider the scalar-valued linear function

\[ Q(\mathbf X,\boldsymbol\beta) =\mathbf X^\top\boldsymbol\beta =X_1\beta_1+X_2\beta_2+\cdots+X_p\beta_p. \]

The gradient with respect to \(\boldsymbol\beta\) is

\[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta} Q(\mathbf X,\boldsymbol\beta) =\begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\beta_1}\\ \displaystyle \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\beta_2}\\ \vdots\\ \displaystyle \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\beta_p} \end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix} X_1\\ X_2\\ \vdots\\ X_p \end{bmatrix} =\mathbf X. \]

Similarly, the gradient with respect to \(\mathbf X\) is

\[ \nabla_{\mathbf X} Q(\mathbf X,\boldsymbol\beta) =\begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle \frac{\partial Q}{\partial X_1}\\ \displaystyle \frac{\partial Q}{\partial X_2}\\ \vdots\\ \displaystyle \frac{\partial Q}{\partial X_p} \end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix} \beta_1\\ \beta_2\\ \vdots\\ \beta_p \end{bmatrix} =\boldsymbol\beta. \]

Thus, the derivative of \(\mathbf X^\top\boldsymbol\beta\) with respect to one vector is the other vector.

Theorem (First-order condition)

To estimate \(\boldsymbol\beta\), we generally solve for all components simultaneously. For an unconstrained interior optimum, the first-order condition is

\[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}Q(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}) =\mathbf 0 \]

This gives a system of \(p\) equations in the \(p\) unknown components of \(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}\).

Definition (Hessian)

As in single-variable optimization, the first-order condition identifies stationary points. Their local curvature is described by the Hessian,

\[ H_Q(\boldsymbol\beta) =\nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}^2Q(\boldsymbol\beta) = \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle \frac{\partial^2Q}{\partial\beta_1^2} & \cdots & \displaystyle \frac{\partial^2Q}{\partial\beta_1\partial\beta_p} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \displaystyle \frac{\partial^2Q}{\partial\beta_p\partial\beta_1} & \cdots & \displaystyle \frac{\partial^2Q}{\partial\beta_p^2} \end{bmatrix}. \]

Theorem (Curvature and optimization)

Suppose \(Q\) is twice continuously differentiable near a stationary point \(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}\). Then,

\[ \begin{aligned} H_Q(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta})\text{ positive definite} &\implies \widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}\text{ is a strict local minimum},\\ H_Q(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta})\text{ negative definite} &\implies \widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}\text{ is a strict local maximum},\\ H_Q(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta})\text{ indefinite} &\implies \widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}\text{ is a saddle point}. \end{aligned} \]

Here positive definiteness means \(\mathbf v^\top H_Q\mathbf v>0\) for every nonzero \(\mathbf v\); negative definiteness reverses the inequality. An indefinite Hessian gives positive curvature in some directions and negative curvature in others. A semidefinite Hessian alone does not settle the classification.

More globally, on an open convex domain, if \(Q\) is convex, any stationary point is a global minimum. If \(Q\) is strictly convex, that minimum is unique. Similarly, if \(Q\) is concave, any stationary point is a global maximum, and strict concavity gives uniqueness.

Example (Least-squares curvature)

For \(Q(\boldsymbol\beta)=\tfrac12\|\mathbf Y-\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2\),

\[ \nabla Q(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf X^\top(\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta-\mathbf Y), \qquad H_Q(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X. \]

For any vector \(\mathbf v\),

\[ \mathbf v^\top H_Q\mathbf v=\|\mathbf X\mathbf v\|^2\ge0. \]

Thus the criterion is convex. If \(\mathbf X\) has full column rank, the Hessian is positive definite and the least-squares solution is unique. This is the multivariable version of a bowl-shaped quadratic.

In statistics, whether we maximize or minimize depends on the criterion. We maximize a log-likelihood or log-posterior, for which negative curvature is desirable, and minimize a loss or negative log-likelihood, for which positive curvature is desirable.

Thus, in statistical applications, the usual sequence is

\[ \text{scalar log-likelihood} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{gradient or score vector} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{Jacobian of the score} =\text{Hessian}. \]

The score helps characterize the estimator through a first-order condition, while the Hessian describes the local curvature of the criterion function and, under standard likelihood regularity conditions, provides an approximation to the estimator’s uncertainty.

In many statistical models, the score equation

\[ \mathbf U_n(\boldsymbol\beta) =\mathbf 0 \]

cannot be solved explicitly. We therefore use Taylor expansions to approximate the score or log-likelihood near a convenient point. In asymptotic statistics, this point is often the true parameter \(\boldsymbol\beta_0\).

As the number of observations \(n\) increases, the score and Hessian accumulate information across observations. In the usual fixed-dimensional parametric setting, the dimension \(p\) of \(\boldsymbol\beta\) remains fixed while \(n\to\infty\). Taylor’s theorem, together with probabilistic order notation, allows us to show that the approximation error becomes negligible relative to the leading terms.

Function Approximation, Taylor’s Theorem, and Little-\(o\)/Big-\(O\) Notation

Write \(\mathbf h\) for a small displacement in parameter space and \(\|\mathbf h\|=(\sum_{j=1}^p h_j^2)^{1/2}\) for its Euclidean length. The scalar displacement \(x-a\) is now replaced by this vector.

Theorem (Multivariable Taylor expansion)

For a differentiable scalar-valued function

\[ Q:\mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R, \]

a first-order Taylor expansion around a point \(\boldsymbol\beta_0\) is

\[ Q(\boldsymbol\beta_0+\mathbf h) =Q(\boldsymbol\beta_0) + \nabla Q(\boldsymbol\beta_0)^\top\mathbf h + r_1(\mathbf h), \]

where the remainder satisfies

\[ r_1(\mathbf h) =o(\|\mathbf h\|) \]

as \(\|\mathbf h\|\to0\). Equivalently,

\[ \frac{r_1(\mathbf h)}{\|\mathbf h\|} \to0. \]

Thus, sufficiently close to \(\boldsymbol\beta_0\), the function is approximately linear:

\[ Q(\boldsymbol\beta_0+\mathbf h) \approx Q(\boldsymbol\beta_0) + \nabla Q(\boldsymbol\beta_0)^\top\mathbf h. \]

If \(Q\) is twice continuously differentiable, then the second-order Taylor expansion is

\[ Q(\boldsymbol\beta_0+\mathbf h) =Q(\boldsymbol\beta_0) + \nabla Q(\boldsymbol\beta_0)^\top\mathbf h + \frac{1}{2} \mathbf h^\top H_Q(\boldsymbol\beta_0) \mathbf h + r_2(\mathbf h), \]

where

\[ r_2(\mathbf h) =o(\|\mathbf h\|^2). \]

The linear term describes the local slope, while the quadratic term describes the local curvature.

Definition (Order notation for vector displacements)

The notation

\[ r(\mathbf h)=o(\|\mathbf h\|^k) \]

means that the remainder is asymptotically negligible relative to \(\|\mathbf h\|^k\):

\[ \frac{r(\mathbf h)}{\|\mathbf h\|^k} \to0. \]

By contrast,

\[ r(\mathbf h)=O(\|\mathbf h\|^k) \]

means that the remainder is bounded in magnitude by a constant multiple of \(\|\mathbf h\|^k\) sufficiently close to zero. That is, there exist constants \(C<\infty\) and \(\delta>0\) such that

\[ |r(\mathbf h)| \le C\|\mathbf h\|^k \]

whenever \(\|\mathbf h\|<\delta\).

Thus, little-\(o\) indicates negligibility relative to a reference rate, whereas big-\(O\) indicates boundedness at that rate.

We now apply these derivatives to likelihood-based estimation.

Definition (Score and information)

For \(n\) independent observations, the log-likelihood can be written as

\[ \ell_n(\boldsymbol\beta) =\sum_{i=1}^n \ell_i(\boldsymbol\beta). \]

Because \(\ell_n(\boldsymbol\beta)\) is scalar-valued, its gradient with respect to \(\boldsymbol\beta\) is the vector of first-order partial derivatives,

\[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ell_n(\boldsymbol\beta) =\begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle \frac{\partial\ell_n(\boldsymbol\beta)}{\partial\beta_1}\\ \displaystyle \frac{\partial\ell_n(\boldsymbol\beta)}{\partial\beta_2}\\ \vdots\\ \displaystyle \frac{\partial\ell_n(\boldsymbol\beta)}{\partial\beta_p} \end{bmatrix}. \]

In likelihood-based inference, this gradient is called the score vector,

\[ \mathbf U_n(\boldsymbol\beta) =\nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ell_n(\boldsymbol\beta). \]

Each component of the score measures how the log-likelihood changes when one parameter varies while the remaining parameters are held fixed. The maximum likelihood estimator is often characterized by the first-order condition

\[ \mathbf U_n(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}) =\nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta} \ell_n(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}) =\mathbf 0 \]

Since the score is a vector-valued function,

\[ \mathbf U_n:\mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R^p, \]

it has a Jacobian. The Jacobian of the score is the Hessian of the log-likelihood:

\[ H_n(\boldsymbol\beta) =\nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}^2 \ell_n(\boldsymbol\beta) =J_{\mathbf U_n}(\boldsymbol\beta). \]

Its entries are the second-order partial derivatives,

\[ H_n(\boldsymbol\beta) =\begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle \frac{\partial^2\ell_n}{\partial\beta_1^2} & \displaystyle \frac{\partial^2\ell_n}{\partial\beta_1\partial\beta_2} & \cdots & \displaystyle \frac{\partial^2\ell_n}{\partial\beta_1\partial\beta_p} \\ \displaystyle \frac{\partial^2\ell_n}{\partial\beta_2\partial\beta_1} & \displaystyle \frac{\partial^2\ell_n}{\partial\beta_2^2} & \cdots & \displaystyle \frac{\partial^2\ell_n}{\partial\beta_2\partial\beta_p} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \displaystyle \frac{\partial^2\ell_n}{\partial\beta_p\partial\beta_1} & \displaystyle \frac{\partial^2\ell_n}{\partial\beta_p\partial\beta_2} & \cdots & \displaystyle \frac{\partial^2\ell_n}{\partial\beta_p^2} \end{bmatrix}. \]

The Hessian describes the local curvature of the log-likelihood with respect to the parameter vector. For a perturbation \(\mathbf h\), the second-order Taylor expansion is

\[ \ell_n(\boldsymbol\beta+\mathbf h) =\ell_n(\boldsymbol\beta) + \mathbf U_n(\boldsymbol\beta)^\top\mathbf h + \frac{1}{2} \mathbf h^\top H_n(\boldsymbol\beta) \mathbf h + o(\|\mathbf h\|^2). \]

Near a maximum likelihood estimator, the log-likelihood typically curves downward, so the Hessian is negative definite or negative semidefinite. Equivalently, the negative Hessian,

\[ -\nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}^2 \ell_n(\boldsymbol\beta), \]

is positive definite or positive semidefinite.

This negative curvature matrix is central to statistical inference. The observed information matrix is

\[ \mathcal J_n(\boldsymbol\beta) =-\nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}^2 \ell_n(\boldsymbol\beta), \]

under the usual regularity conditions, the Fisher information matrix is

\[ \mathcal I_n(\boldsymbol\beta) =-E_{\boldsymbol\beta} \left[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta}^2 \ell_n(\boldsymbol\beta) \right]. \]

The score equation is vector-valued, so its exact expansion uses the Jacobian integrated along the line segment between the two parameter values. In general, there need not be a single intermediate point that works for every component.

Theorem (Exact score expansion and MLE approximation)

Suppose the log-likelihood is twice continuously differentiable along the segment from \(\boldsymbol\beta_0\) to an interior MLE \(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}\). Define

\[ \overline H_n =\int_0^1 H_n\bigl(\boldsymbol\beta_0+t(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}-\boldsymbol\beta_0)\bigr)\,dt. \]

Applying the exact Taylor representation componentwise to the score gives

\[ \mathbf 0=\mathbf U_n(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}) =\mathbf U_n(\boldsymbol\beta_0) +\overline H_n(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}-\boldsymbol\beta_0). \]

If \(\overline H_n\) is invertible, rearranging yields

\[ \widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}-\boldsymbol\beta_0 =-\overline H_n^{-1}\mathbf U_n(\boldsymbol\beta_0). \]

Multiplying by \(\sqrt n\) and rescaling gives

\[ \sqrt n(\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}-\boldsymbol\beta_0) =\left[-\frac{1}{n}\overline H_n\right]^{-1} \frac{1}{\sqrt n}\mathbf U_n(\boldsymbol\beta_0). \]

For a correctly specified, regular i.i.d. model with fixed parameter dimension, the two main asymptotic ingredients are

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt n}\mathbf U_n(\boldsymbol\beta_0) \overset{d}{\longrightarrow} N\bigl(\mathbf 0,\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\beta_0)\bigr) \]

and

\[ -\frac{1}{n}\overline H_n \overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal I(\boldsymbol\beta_0), \]

where \(\mathcal I\) is the information per observation and is assumed positive definite. The first convergence follows from a multivariate central limit theorem for the score; the second requires consistency and suitable local uniform control of the Hessian, not just a pointwise law of large numbers.

Slutsky’s theorem then gives

\[ \sqrt n \left( \widehat{\boldsymbol\beta} -\boldsymbol\beta_0 \right) \overset{d}{\longrightarrow} N\left( \mathbf 0, \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\beta_0)^{-1} \right). \]

Therefore,

\[ \widehat{\boldsymbol\beta} \approx N\left( \boldsymbol\beta_0, \frac{1}{n} \mathcal I(\boldsymbol\beta_0)^{-1} \right) \]

for large \(n\). With the additional moment conditions needed to approximate variances,

\[ \operatorname{Var} \left( \widehat{\boldsymbol\beta} \right) \approx \mathcal I_n(\boldsymbol\beta_0)^{-1}. \]

Thus, Taylor expansion converts the nonlinear score equation into an approximately linear equation, while asymptotic arguments control the approximation error and determine the limiting distribution of the estimator.

Example (Normal mean: an exact score expansion)

Let \(X_i\) be i.i.d. \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) with known \(\sigma^2\). Then

\[ U_n(\mu)=\frac{n}{\sigma^2}(\bar X-\mu), \qquad H_n(\mu)=-\frac{n}{\sigma^2}. \]

The Hessian is constant, so the score expansion gives exactly

\[ \widehat\mu-\mu_0 =-\left(-\frac{n}{\sigma^2}\right)^{-1} \frac{n}{\sigma^2}(\bar X-\mu_0) =\bar X-\mu_0. \]

Here \(\mathcal I_n=n/\sigma^2\), and its inverse \(\sigma^2/n\) is the exact variance of \(\widehat\mu=\bar X\).

Directional Derivatives

Suppose we wanted to describe the rate of change of a function in an arbitrary direction in its input or parameter space. Partial derivatives describe this rate along the coordinate directions: each varies one coordinate while holding the others fixed. A directional derivative allows several coordinates to change together, in proportions specified by a direction vector.

To define it, start at \(\boldsymbol\beta\) and move along a unit vector \(\mathbf v\). The perturbed input is \(\boldsymbol\beta+t\mathbf v\), where \(t\) is the signed distance moved. Applying the usual derivative definition to this one-dimensional path gives the directional derivative.

Definition (Directional derivative and gradient)

For a differentiable function \(Q:\mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R\) and a unit vector \(\mathbf v=(v_1,\ldots,v_p)^\top\), the directional derivative at \(\boldsymbol\beta\) is

\[ D_{\mathbf v}Q(\boldsymbol\beta) \equiv \lim_{t\to0} \frac{Q(\boldsymbol\beta+t\mathbf v)-Q(\boldsymbol\beta)}{t} = \left.\frac{d}{dt}Q(\boldsymbol\beta+t\mathbf v)\right|_{t=0}. \]

Along this path, coordinate \(j\) changes at rate \(v_j\). By the chain rule,

\[ D_{\mathbf v}Q(\boldsymbol\beta) = \sum_{j=1}^p \frac{\partial Q(\boldsymbol\beta)}{\partial\beta_j}\,v_j. \]

Thus, the directional derivative is a sum of partial derivatives, each multiplied by the rate at which its coordinate changes. Collecting the partial derivatives into a vector defines the gradient:

\[ \nabla Q(\boldsymbol\beta) \equiv \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial Q(\boldsymbol\beta)}{\partial\beta_1}\\ \vdots\\ \frac{\partial Q(\boldsymbol\beta)}{\partial\beta_p} \end{pmatrix}. \]

The directional derivative is therefore the inner product

\[ D_{\mathbf v}Q(\boldsymbol\beta) = \nabla Q(\boldsymbol\beta)^\top\mathbf v. \]

Choosing \(\mathbf v=\mathbf e_j\), the \(j\)th coordinate unit vector, recovers the \(j\)th partial derivative. More generally, the gradient determines the slope in every direction. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\[ D_{\mathbf v}Q(\boldsymbol\beta) \leq \|\nabla Q(\boldsymbol\beta)\|\,\|\mathbf v\| = \|\nabla Q(\boldsymbol\beta)\|. \]

When the gradient is nonzero, equality holds for \(\mathbf v=\nabla Q(\boldsymbol\beta)/\|\nabla Q(\boldsymbol\beta)\|\). The gradient therefore points in the direction of greatest local increase, and its norm gives that maximum rate of increase.

Example (Normal Scores)

Suppose \[ X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2), \] where \(\mu\) is the target and \(\sigma^2\) is the nuisance. Write \(v=\sigma^2\) to simplify the notation.

A path through this model is \[ \mu_t=\mu+ta,\qquad v_t=v+tb. \] The vector \((a,b)\) specifies the direction: how quickly the mean and variance change. For sufficiently small \(|t|\), the variance remains positive.

The log density is \[ \log p_{\mu,v}(x) = -\frac12\log(2\pi v)-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2v}. \]

Applying the directional-derivative formula gives \[ \begin{aligned} s_{a,b}(x) &=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\log p_{\mu+ta,v+tb}(x)\right|_{t=0}\\ &=a\frac{\partial\log p_{\mu,v}(x)}{\partial\mu} +b\frac{\partial\log p_{\mu,v}(x)}{\partial v}\\ &=a\underbrace{\frac{x-\mu}{v}}_{\text{mean score}} +b\underbrace{\frac{(x-\mu)^2-v}{2v^2}}_{\text{variance score}}. \end{aligned} \]

To recover the usual normal scores set either \((1,0)\) or \((0,1)\)

Connecting Calculus to Statistics

In statistics we deal with special functions that distribute the “mass” of some random process across possible outcomes, called probability distributions. Calculus underlies both the way we compute probabilities (via integration) and the way we estimate parameters (via differentiation).

Generally we define these functions as probability density function (PDF) for continuous variables or probability mass functions (PMF) for discrete, briefly these can be both be defined using integration where the discrete (PMF) and continuous (PDF) cases are only distinguished by the measure used - the discrete cases use a counting measure and the integral becomes a sum.

Definition

A measurable function \(f(x)\) is a probability density function with respect to Lebesgue measure on a measurable set \(S\) if

  1. \(f(x)\ge0\) for all \(x\in S\),
  2. \(\displaystyle\int_S f(x)dx = 1\)

If \(S\) contains subintervals, \([a,b]\subset S\), \[ \Pr(a \le X \le b) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x)dx \]

You can integrate the pdf \(f_X\) to get a cumulative distribution function (CDF) \(F_X\) with the following properties,

  • \(F_X\) is nondecreasing: if \(x_1<x_2\) then \(F_X(x_1)\le F_X(x_2)\).
  • Right-continuous: \(\displaystyle \lim_{h\downarrow0}F_X(x+h)=F_X(x)\) for all \(x\).
  • Boundary limits: \(\displaystyle \lim_{x\to-\infty}F_X(x)=0\) and \(\displaystyle \lim_{x\to+\infty}F_X(x)=1\).

Every real-valued random variable has a CDF, which uniquely determines its distribution; not every distribution has a PDF or PMF. For more details, please see the probability guide.

From Positive Functions to a PDF

We can start with a simple nonnegative function on an interval and “rescale” it so that its total area is 1 and it acts as PDF,

Example

Take \[ g(x) = x^2,\qquad x\in[0,1]. \] Its total area is \[ \int_{0}^{1} x^2 \ dx = \Bigl[\tfrac{x^3}{3}\Bigr]_{0}^{1} = \tfrac13. \] To make a PDF \(f(x)\), divide by this area: \[ f(x) = \frac{g(x)}{\int_0^1 g(t)dt} = \frac{x^2}{1/3} = 3x^2, \quad x\in[0,1]. \] One checks \[ \int_{0}^{1} 3x^2dx = 3\cdot\frac13 = 1, \] so \(f\) is a valid PDF.

We usually deal with a set of standard PDF functions that describe specific types of situations given below,

Optimization

Definition (Likelihood and maximum likelihood estimation)

In statistical inference, we typically assume we have observed data points \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) that are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) from a common probability density function \(f(x;\theta)\), where \(\theta\) is an unknown parameter (or vector of parameters). Under the i.i.d. assumption, the joint density of the sample is \[ L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i;\theta), \] which we refer to as the likelihood function. To estimate \(\theta\), we select the value \(\hat\theta\) that maximizes this joint density:

\[ \hat\theta = \arg\max_{\theta} L(\theta), \] or equivalently, since the logarithm is a strictly increasing function, \[ \hat\theta = \arg\max_{\theta} \ell(\theta), \quad \ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log f(x_i;\theta). \] This procedure is known as the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE).

Remark

MLE is nothing more than the optimization from regular calculus, but now maximizing the above \(L(\theta)\), or equivalently \(\ell(\theta)\), with respect to \(\theta\). Therefore we must also check concavity and boundary behavior (if it exists)

R can make this maximization relatively easy using the optim function, (whose default is minimization so for maximum likelihood we equivalently minimize the negative log-likelihood,)

Closed-Form Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Some maximum likelihood estimators can be obtained directly by differentiating the log-likelihood, setting the resulting score equation equal to zero, and solving analytically for the unknown parameter.

Example (Normal regression and ordinary least squares)

For example, consider the normal linear regression model

\[ Y_i\mid X_i \sim N(X_i^\top\beta,\sigma^2), \]

or, in matrix form,

\[ \mathbf Y\mid \mathbf X \sim N_n(\mathbf X\beta,\sigma^2\mathbf I_n), \]

where \(\mathbf X\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}\). The likelihood for \(\beta\) is

\[ L(\beta;\mathbf Y,\mathbf X) =\prod_{i=1}^n (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2} \exp\left\{ -\frac{(Y_i-X_i^\top\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right\}, \]

and the corresponding log-likelihood is

\[ \ell(\beta;\mathbf Y,\mathbf X) =\sum_{i=1}^n \left[ -\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) -\frac{(Y_i-X_i^\top\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right] \]

Equivalently, using matrix notation,

\[ \ell(\beta;\mathbf Y,\mathbf X) =-\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} (\mathbf Y-\mathbf X\beta)^\top (\mathbf Y-\mathbf X\beta). \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\beta\) gives the score vector

\[ s(\beta) =\frac{\partial\ell(\beta)}{\partial\beta} =\frac{1}{\sigma^2} \mathbf X^\top (\mathbf Y-\mathbf X\beta). \]

Setting the score equal to zero yields the normal equations,

\[ \mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\beta =\mathbf X^\top\mathbf Y. \]

Provided that \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\) is invertible, the maximum likelihood estimator is therefore

\[ \widehat\beta_{\mathrm{OLS}} =(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1} \mathbf X^\top\mathbf Y. \]

Example (Generalized least squares)

More generally, suppose the errors are correlated or have unequal variances, so that

\[ \mathbf Y\mid\mathbf X \sim N_n(\mathbf X\beta,\boldsymbol\Sigma), \]

where \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) is a known positive-definite covariance matrix that need not be diagonal. The log-likelihood is then, up to terms not involving \(\beta\),

\[ \ell(\beta) =-\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf Y-\mathbf X\beta)^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf Y-\mathbf X\beta), \]

with score vector

\[ s(\beta)=\nabla \ell(\beta) =\mathbf X^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf Y-\mathbf X\beta). \]

Setting this score equal to zero gives the generalized least-squares estimator

\[ \widehat\beta_{\mathrm{GLS}} =\left( \mathbf X^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} \mathbf X \right)^{-1} \mathbf X^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} \mathbf Y. \]

Ordinary least squares is the special case in which

\[ \boldsymbol\Sigma=\sigma^2\mathbf I_n. \]

Thus, although these estimators require matrix calculations, they can be obtained in a single analytical step rather than through an iterative numerical optimization procedure.

Iterative Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Many likelihoods do not yield closed-form estimators and must instead be maximized iteratively. Logistic regression provides an important example.

Example (Logistic regression score)

Suppose

\[ Y_i\mid X_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_i), \]

with the logit link

\[ \log\left(\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}\right) =X_i^\top\beta. \]

Equivalently,

\[ p_i(\beta) =\frac{\exp(X_i^\top\beta)} {1+\exp(X_i^\top\beta)}. \]

The likelihood for \(\beta\) is therefore

\[ L(\beta) =\prod_{i=1}^n p_i(\beta)^{Y_i} {1-p_i(\beta)}^{1-Y_i}, \]

and the corresponding log-likelihood is

\[ \ell(\beta) =\sum_{i=1}^n \left[ Y_iX_i^\top\beta -\log\left\{ 1+\exp(X_i^\top\beta) \right\} \right]. \]

Differentiating with respect to \(\beta\) gives the score vector,

\[ \begin{aligned} s(\beta) =\frac{\partial\ell(\beta)}{\partial\beta} &= \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ Y_iX_i -\frac{X_i\exp(X_i^\top\beta)} {1+\exp(X_i^\top\beta)} \right] \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n X_i{Y_i-p_i(\beta)}. \end{aligned} \]

In matrix notation, this is

\[ s(\beta) =\mathbf X^\top {\mathbf Y-\mathbf p(\beta)}. \]

We want to choose \(\beta\) so that

\[ s(\beta)=0. \]

Unlike the normal linear-model score equation, however, this equation is nonlinear in \(\beta\) because \(\mathbf p(\beta)\) depends nonlinearly on \(\beta\). It therefore cannot generally be rearranged to produce an explicit formula for \(\widehat\beta\).

Theorem (Newton–Raphson for logistic regression)

Instead, we begin with an initial estimate \(\beta^{(0)}\), approximate the score or log-likelihood locally, update the estimate, and repeat until the estimates stabilize. Newton–Raphson uses both the score and the Hessian. For logistic regression, the Hessian is

\[ H(\beta) =\frac{\partial^2\ell(\beta)} {\partial\beta\partial\beta^\top} =-\mathbf X^\top \mathbf W(\beta) \mathbf X, \]

where

\[ \mathbf W(\beta) =\operatorname{diag} \left\{ p_i(\beta) \bigl[1-p_i(\beta)\bigr] \right\}_{i=1}^n. \]

The Newton–Raphson update is therefore

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta^{(t+1)} &= \beta^{(t)} -H\left(\beta^{(t)}\right)^{-1} s\left(\beta^{(t)}\right) \\ &= \beta^{(t)} + \left( \mathbf X^\top \mathbf W^{(t)} \mathbf X \right)^{-1} \mathbf X^\top \left( \mathbf Y-\mathbf p^{(t)} \right), \end{aligned} \]

where

\[ \mathbf p^{(t)} =\mathbf p\left(\beta^{(t)}\right) \]

and

\[ \mathbf W^{(t)} =\mathbf W\left(\beta^{(t)}\right). \]

For logistic regression, the negative Hessian is also the Fisher information matrix because it does not depend directly on the observed values of \(\mathbf Y\). Thus, Newton–Raphson and Fisher scoring produce the same update.

Remark (Iteratively reweighted least squares)

This update can be rewritten as a weighted least-squares problem. Define the current linear predictor

\[ \boldsymbol\eta^{(t)} =\mathbf X\beta^{(t)} \]

and the working response

\[ \mathbf z^{(t)} =\boldsymbol\eta^{(t)} + \left(\mathbf W^{(t)}\right)^{-1} \left( \mathbf Y-\mathbf p^{(t)} \right). \]

Then the next estimate satisfies

\[ \beta^{(t+1)} =\left( \mathbf X^\top \mathbf W^{(t)} \mathbf X \right)^{-1} \mathbf X^\top \mathbf W^{(t)} \mathbf z^{(t)}. \]

This has exactly the form of a weighted least-squares estimator. The procedure is therefore called iteratively reweighted least squares, or IRLS:

  1. Use the current estimate \(\beta^{(t)}\) to calculate the fitted probabilities \(\mathbf p^{(t)}\).
  2. Use those probabilities to calculate the weights \(\mathbf W^{(t)}\) and working response \(\mathbf z^{(t)}\).
  3. Regress the working response on \(\mathbf X\) using weighted least squares.
  4. Repeat until the estimates change negligibly.

The intuition is that logistic regression is nonlinear globally but can be approximated locally by a weighted linear regression. At each iteration, the algorithm constructs a temporary linear-regression problem around the current estimate. The weights are updated because the Bernoulli variance,

\[ p_i(\beta){1-p_i(\beta)}, \]

depends on the current fitted probability. Consequently, each iteration resembles the normal equations from linear regression, but both the working outcome and the observation weights change as the algorithm moves toward the maximum likelihood estimate.

Asymptotics

Taylor expansions are the workhorses of asymptotic statistical inference,

Definition (Probabilistic order notation)

Thus, little-\(o_p\) means negligible relative to the reference rate in probability, while big-\(O_p\) means bounded at that rate in probability. These statements do not require a bound that holds for every possible sample.

Theorem (First-order delta method)

Suppose \(\widehat\theta\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\theta_0\) and \(f\) is differentiable at \(\theta_0\). Differentiability gives

\[ f(\widehat\theta) =f(\theta_0)+f'(\theta_0)(\widehat\theta-\theta_0) +(\widehat\theta-\theta_0)\varepsilon_n, \qquad \varepsilon_n=o_p(1). \]

If \(\widehat\theta-\theta_0=O_p(n^{-1/2})\), then

\[ f(\widehat\theta)-f(\theta_0) =f'(\theta_0)(\widehat\theta-\theta_0)+o_p(n^{-1/2}). \]

In particular, if

\[ \sqrt n(\widehat\theta-\theta_0)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N(0,V), \]

then

\[ \sqrt n\{f(\widehat\theta)-f(\theta_0)\} \overset{d}{\longrightarrow} N\bigl(0,[f'(\theta_0)]^2V\bigr). \]

The remainder disappears on the \(\sqrt n\) scale. When \(f'(\theta_0)=0\), this limit is degenerate, and a higher-order expansion may be needed.

Example (Log odds of an estimated probability)

Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) be independent Bernoulli\((p)\) observations with \(0<p<1\) and \(\widehat p=\bar X\). The central limit theorem gives

\[ \sqrt n(\widehat p-p)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N\{0,p(1-p)\}. \]

For \(f(p)=\log\{p/(1-p)\}\), the derivative is \(f'(p)=1/\{p(1-p)\}\). Therefore,

\[ \sqrt n\left\{ \log\frac{\widehat p}{1-\widehat p} -\log\frac{p}{1-p} \right\} \overset{d}{\longrightarrow} N\left(0,\frac{1}{p(1-p)}\right). \]

For an interior estimate, the approximate standard error of the estimated log odds is \(\{n\widehat p(1-\widehat p)\}^{-1/2}\). The cases \(\widehat p=0\) or \(1\) have probability tending to zero; the log odds need a separate convention on those samples.

This separation into a linear term and a negligible remainder is the basic mechanism behind the delta method, asymptotic linearity, influence-function calculations, and many empirical-process arguments.

Examples

Example (Uniform\((0,\theta)\) maximization)

Let \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) be i.i.d. \(\mathrm{Uniform}(0,\theta)\) with \(\theta>0\). Then the joint density is \[ L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\theta} = \theta^{-n}, \qquad \theta \ge X_{(n)} =\max_{1\le i\le n}X_i, \] and \(L(\theta)=0\) for \(\theta<X_{(n)}\). The log‐likelihood is \[ \ell(\theta) = -n\ln\theta, \qquad \theta \ge X_{(n)}. \] Differentiating, \[ \frac{d\ell}{d\theta} = -\frac{n}{\theta} <0 \quad\text{for }\theta>X_{(n)}, \] so there is no interior critical point in \((X_{(n)},\infty)\). Since \(\ell(\theta)\) is strictly decreasing on its domain, the maximum occurs at the boundary: \[ \hat\theta = X_{(n)}\quad \text{ (max observed data point)} \] Thus the MLE lies on the boundary of the region where the likelihood is positive, and one must explicitly check the endpoint \(\theta = X_{(n)}\) to locate the maximum.

Example (Binomial likelihood maximization)

Suppose \(X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)\) and we observe \(0<x<n\) successes in \(n\) trials. The likelihood is \[ L(p) = \Pr(X=x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}, \quad p\in(0,1). \] The log-likelihood is \[ \ell(p) = \log L(p) = \log\binom{n}{x} + x\log p +(n-x)\log(1-p). \] Differentiate and set to zero: \[ \frac{d\ell}{dp} = \frac{x}{p} - \frac{n-x}{1-p} =0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{x}{p} = \frac{n-x}{1-p} \Longrightarrow \hat p = \frac{x}{n}. \] One checks \(\dfrac{d^2\ell}{dp^2}<0\) for \(p\in(0,1)\), so \(\hat p=x/n\) is the unique maximizer. If the parameter space includes \(0\) and \(1\), samples with \(x=0\) or \(x=n\) give boundary estimates \(0\) or \(1\), respectively.

Example (Poisson likelihood maximization)

Let \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) be i.i.d. \(\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)\) and observe data \(x_1,\dots,x_n\). The joint likelihood is \[ L(\lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{\lambda^{x_i}e^{-\lambda}}{x_i!} = \lambda^{\sum_i x_i} e^{-n\lambda} \prod_{i=1}^n\frac{1}{x_i!}. \] The log-likelihood (up to additive constant) is \[ \ell(\lambda) = \Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\Bigr)\log\lambda - n\lambda. \] Differentiate and set to zero: \[ \frac{d\ell}{d\lambda} = \frac{\sum_i x_i}{\lambda} - n =0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\lambda = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i. \] When \(\sum_i x_i>0\), \(\dfrac{d^2\ell}{d\lambda^2}<0\), so \(\hat\lambda=\bar X\) is the MLE. If all counts are zero and \(\lambda=0\) is allowed, the maximum occurs at that boundary.

Example (Normal likelihood maximization)

Let \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) be i.i.d. \(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\), with \(n\ge2\) and nonconstant observed data. The log-likelihood (up to constants) is \[ \ell(\mu,\sigma) = -n\ln\sigma - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\mu)^2. \] Differentiating: \[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\mu) \Longrightarrow \hat\mu = \bar x, \] \[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\sigma} = -\frac{n}{\sigma} + \frac{1}{\sigma^3}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\mu)^2 \Longrightarrow \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)^2. \]

Countable families and nested sets

A set is countable if it is finite or its elements can be listed in a sequence indexed by the positive integers. The integers are countable: start with \(0,1,-1,2,-2,\ldots\). The interval \([0,1]\) is uncountable. No such list contains every point in it. Indexed unions and intersections make sense in either case, but a sequence \((A_n)_{n\ge1}\) gives us a specific order in which to consider the sets.

If \(A_1\subset A_2\subset\cdots\) and \(A=\bigcup_{n\ge1}A_n\), we write \(A_n\uparrow A\). Once an outcome enters the sequence, it stays. If \(B_1\supseteq B_2\supseteq\cdots\) and \(B=\bigcap_{n\ge1}B_n\), we write \(B_n\downarrow B\). An outcome belongs to \(B\) precisely when it survives every step.

For example, take \(A_n\) to be the closed disk of radius \(n\) centered at the origin in the plane, and put \(A_0=\varnothing\). Then \(A_n\uparrow\mathbb R^2\). Each difference \(D_n=A_n\setminus A_{n-1}\) is the part added at step \(n\): first a disk, then successive rings. Those pieces are pairwise disjoint, and \[ A_n=\bigcup_{k=1}^n D_k,\qquad \mathbb R^2=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} D_k. \] For a decreasing example, \([-1/n,1/n]\downarrow\{0\}\). Every interval contains zero, and every nonzero point eventually falls outside. In the Advanced section we drop the nesting assumption and ask whether a point belongs to infinitely many sets or to every set from some point onward.

When membership can change more than once

Fix the sample space of a die. At odd indices let \(A_n=\{2,4,6\}\), and at even indices let \(A_n=\{4\}\). Follow the roll \(2\). However far out in the sequence you start, it belongs to some later set. Yet there is no point after which it belongs to every set. The roll \(4\), on the other hand, belongs to every set from the start.

Infinitely often and eventually always

Starting at index \(m\), the tail union \(\bigcup_{n\ge m}A_n\) contains outcomes that appear at least once from \(m\) onward. Requiring this for every starting index gives the upper limit: \[ \limsup_{n\to\infty} A_n =\bigcap_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{n\ge m} A_n. \] An outcome belongs to this set exactly when it belongs to \(A_n\) infinitely often.

The tail intersection \(\bigcap_{n\ge m}A_n\) contains outcomes that stay from \(m\) onward. Requiring this for some starting index gives the lower limit: \[ \liminf_{n\to\infty} A_n =\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigcap_{n\ge m} A_n. \] An outcome belongs to this set exactly when it belongs to \(A_n\) eventually always. In the die example, the upper limit is \(\{2,4,6\}\) and the lower limit is \(\{4\}\).

An outcome that eventually stays must appear infinitely often, so \(\liminf_n A_n\subset\limsup_n A_n\). Their difference consists of outcomes that keep entering and leaving. When the two limits agree, we say the sets converge to their common limit. For an increasing sequence, both limits equal the union: once an outcome enters, it stays. For a decreasing sequence, both equal the intersection.

A donut argument

Return to the increasing disks from Essential. Let \(A_n\) be the closed disk of radius \(n\), put \(A_0=\varnothing\), and consider just the newly added ring \(D_n=A_n\setminus A_{n-1}\). Although the expanding disks cover the entire plane, each point belongs to exactly one ring. What is \(\limsup_n D_n\)?

Fix any point and let \(k\) be the index of its ring. The tail starting at \(k+1\) never contains that point. Since every point misses some tail union, \(\limsup_n D_n=\varnothing\). The disks and the rings have the same union, but very different upper limits. A union asks whether a point appears even once. An upper limit asks whether it appears however far out we start.
