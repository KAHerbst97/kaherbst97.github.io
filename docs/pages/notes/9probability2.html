Probability Theory, Statistical Models, and Setting Up Inference

Probability theory provides the mathematical foundation for statistical modeling. It defines random variables, probability distributions, conditional distributions, expectations, and dependence, allowing uncertainty to be represented mathematically. It also describes how random data behave under repeated sampling through results such as the law of large numbers and central limit theorem.

A useful distinction is that probability and statistics operate in opposite directions. Probability is the forward problem: given a probability distribution or data-generating mechanism, say \(P_0=P_{\theta_0}\), determine the behavior of observable random variables and statistics generated under \(P_0\). Statistics is the inverse problem: given observed data generated from some unknown \(P_0\), use the statistical model \(\mathcal P\) and the data to learn about \(P_0\) or particular features of \(P_0\).

Schematically,

\[ \text{Probability: } P_0 \longrightarrow \text{distribution of observable data}, \]

whereas

\[ \text{Statistics: } \text{observed data} \longrightarrow \text{inference about }P_0 \text{ or a functional of }P_0. \]

The role of the statistical model \(\mathcal P\) is therefore to specify the collection of probability distributions regarded as plausible data-generating mechanisms, while \(P_0\in\mathcal P\) denotes the true, generally unknown, data-generating distribution.

We focus below on the probability and measure-theoretic foundations needed for parametric statistical modeling. The forward-versus-inverse distinction will become increasingly concrete as we develop random variables, distributions, likelihoods, and statistical inference.

Conditioning, Densities, and Changes of Measure

Conditional probability can also be understood as a change of probability measure. Rather than assigning probabilities over the entire sample space \(\Omega\), conditioning on an event \(B\) restricts our attention to \(B\) and renormalizes the probabilities so that \(B\) has probability one.

Formally, given a probability space \((\Omega,\mathcal F,P)\) and an event \(B\) with \(P(B)>0\), define the conditional probability measure

\[ P_B(A)\coloneqq P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}. \]

This new measure satisfies \(P_B(B)=1\) and \(P_B(B^c)=0\). Moreover, \(P_B\) is absolutely continuous with respect to \(P\), and its Radon–Nikodym derivative is

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dP_B}{dP}(\omega) =\frac{\mathbf 1_B(\omega)}{P(B)}. } \]

Consequently, conditional probability can be expressed as an integral under the original measure:

\[ \begin{aligned} P(A\mid B) &=\int_A\frac{dP_B}{dP}\,dP\\ &=\frac{1}{P(B)}\int_A\mathbf 1_B\,dP\\ &=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}. \end{aligned} \]

The same principle appears when working with conditional densities. If \((X,Y)\) has joint density \(f_{X,Y}\) and marginal density \(f_Y\), then, for \(f_Y(y)>0\),

\[ \boxed{ f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y) =\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}. } \]

Intuitively, we restrict the joint distribution to a particular value of \(Y\) and normalize by its marginal density. Unlike conditioning on an event with positive probability, however, conditioning on \(Y=y\) for a continuous random variable requires a conditional distribution, since \(P(Y=y)=0\).

More generally, conditioning on a \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal F\) is formalized through the Radon–Nikodym theorem. For an event \(A\), define \(\nu_A(G)=P(A\cap G)\) for \(G\in\mathcal G\). Then

\[ P(A\mid\mathcal G) =\frac{d\nu_A}{d(P|_{\mathcal G})} =E[\mathbf 1_A\mid\mathcal G]. \]

Thus, conditional probability, conditional densities, and conditional expectation are closely connected through restricting, normalizing, and disintegrating probability measures.

Definition: Mean/Expectation

The mean or expectation of a random variable \(X\) is its probability-weighted average. The same quantity can be expressed in several useful ways: by averaging values, averaging quantiles, accumulating tail probabilities, or first conditioning on another variable.

Assume \(\mathbb{E}[|X|]<\infty\), and let \(F_X\) denote the CDF of \(X\).

Representation Expression Conditions or interpretation
Probability space \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_\Omega X(\omega)\,dP(\omega)\) The underlying measure-theoretic definition
Distribution \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,dF_X(x)\) Average values according to the distribution of \(X\)
Discrete \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}}x\,p_X(x)\) \(p_X(x)=P(X=x)\) is the probability mass function
Density \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x f_X(x)\,dx\) Applies when \(X\) has a density \(f_X\)
Quantiles \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_0^1F_X^{-1}(u)\,du\) Uniformly average all quantile levels
General tails \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_0^\infty P(X>t)\,dt-\int_0^\infty P(X<-t)\,dt\) Expected positive part minus expected negative part
CDF areas \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_0^\infty[1-F_X(t)]\,dt-\int_{-\infty}^0F_X(t)\,dt\) Express the tail integrals using the CDF
Iterated expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]]\) Average conditional means over the distribution of \(Y\)
Change of measure \(\mathbb{E}_P[X]=\mathbb{E}_Q[X\,dP/dQ]\) Requires \(P\ll Q\); reweight an expectation under another probability measure
Characteristic function \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\varphi_X'(0)/i\), where \(\varphi_X(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]\) Recover the mean from the derivative at zero

Here \(F_X^{-1}(u)=\inf\{x:F_X(x)\ge u\}\) is the generalized quantile function, so the quantile representation also applies to discrete and mixed distributions. For nonnegative \(X\), the nonnegative integral and sum representations remain valid when \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\infty\).

A related optimization characterization, assuming \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]<\infty\), is \[ \boxed{\mathbb{E}[X]=\underset{a\in\mathbb{R}}{\arg\min}\;\mathbb{E}[(X-a)^2].} \] Thus, the mean is the constant that minimizes expected squared prediction error.

A Short Overview of Empirical Processes

A statistical model \(\mathcal P\) is a collection of probability measures \(\mathbb P\) defined on a common measurable space \((\Omega,\Sigma)\). Each \(\mathbb P\in\mathcal P\) is itself a set function \(\mathbb P:\Sigma\to[0,1]\). Thus, fixing the measurable space \((\Omega,\Sigma)\), the model determines a collection of probability spaces \(\{(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb P):\mathbb P\in\mathcal P\}\).

Now fix a real-valued random variable \(X\), that is, a measurable map \(X:(\Omega,\Sigma)\to(\mathbb R,\mathcal B(\mathbb R))\). Each \(\mathbb P\in\mathcal P\) induces a probability distribution for \(X\) given by \(P_X=\mathbb P\circ X^{-1}\). For some fixed but unknown \(\mathbb P\in\mathcal P\), the corresponding induced distribution \(P_X\) is likewise fixed but unknown. We take an i.i.d. sample from \(P_X\):

\[X_1,\ldots,X_n\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}P_X\]

Before observation, \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are random variables. After observation, their realized values are denoted by \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\). We then use the observed sample \((x_i)_{i=1}^n\) to infer properties of the true distribution \(P_X\).

Example: ECDF as NPMLE for CDF

Estimating the CDF is a natural starting point because \(P_X\) is uniquely characterized by its CDF,

\[ F_X(t) = \mathbb P(X\leq t) = P_X((-\infty,t]). \]

This problem seems particularly daunting under such an expansive statistical model as the collection of all probability measures \(P\) on \(\mathbb R\), because each possible \(P\) has its own cumulative distribution function

\[ F_P(t) = P((-\infty,t]), \qquad t\in\mathbb R. \]

Suppose the observed values \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) are distinct. Then the nonparametric likelihood is the product

\[ L(Q;x_1,\ldots,x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n Q(\{x_i\}) = \prod_{i=1}^n p_i, \]

where \(p_i=Q(\{x_i\})\) is some candidate distribution. At the maximizer, all probability mass is assigned to the observed values, so \(\sum_{i=1}^n p_i=1\). Then, by the arithmetic mean-geometric mean inequality,

\[\begin{align*} \left(\prod_{i=1}^n p_i\right)^{1/n} &\leq \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n p_i =\frac{1}{n},\\ \prod_{i=1}^n p_i &\leq \frac{1}{n^n}. \end{align*}\]

Equality holds exactly when

\[ p_i=\frac{1}{n}, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n. \]

Thus, the NPMLE of \(P_X\) assigns estimated point mass \(\widehat P_X({x_i})=1/n\) to each observed value. The NPMLE of the CDF, called the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF), then simply keeps track of how many of the observed values \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) are less than or equal to a threshold \(t\):

\[ F_n(t) = \widehat F_X(t) = \widehat P_X((-\infty,t]) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf 1\{x_i\leq t\}. \]

What are Empirical Processes?: A Functional Perspective

Example: ECDF as a Random Empirical Function

Thinking more generally, the ECDF \(F_n\) can be viewed as a random function obtained by empirically averaging functions from the deterministic indicator class

\[ \mathcal F_{\mathrm{CDF}} = \left\{ f_t:f_t(x)=\mathbf 1\{x\leq t\},\ t\in\mathbb R \right\}. \]

The empirical measure \(P_n\) acts as the empirical-average operator \(f\mapsto P_n f\). Therefore, for each \(f_t\in\mathcal F_{\mathrm{CDF}}\), we can write

\[ F_n(t) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f_t(X_i) = P_n f_t \]

The functions \(f_t\) themselves are not random. The ECDF is random because the empirical-average operator \(P_n\) depends on the random observations \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\). Equivalently, for each \(i\), the mapping \(f_t:t\mapsto\mathbf 1\{X_i\leq t\}\) is a random step function, and \(F_n\) is the average of these random functions.

The empirical distribution is an equally weighted sum (mixture) of Dirac probability measures, each concentrated at one observed data point.

For observations \(X_1,,X_n\),

Each \(_{X_i}\) assigns probability \(1\) to \(X_i\), and multiplying by \(1/n\) assigns each observation probability mass \(1/n\).

For any measurable set \(A\),

\[ P_n(A)=1n_{i=1}^n(X_iA). \]

Thus, \(P_n(A)\) is simply the proportion of observations belonging to \(A\).

This is why empirical process theory expresses sample averages as integrals with respect to the empirical distribution.

Strictly speaking, these are Dirac measures, rather than ordinary probability density functions.

As with the ECDF, the empirical-average operator can be used to define many common statistics, and empirical-process theory studies the asymptotic behavior of these empirical averages directly. Writing \(P=P_X\) for the true distribution of \(X\), the empirical measure is

\[ P_n=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{X_i} \]

For a measurable and \(P\)-integrable function \(f\), the empirical and population averages are

\[ P_n f = \int f\,dP_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i), \qquad Pf = E_P[f(X)] \]

A statistical procedure typically determines a particular class of functions \(\mathcal F\) that is evaluated using the empirical measure

Example: Common Statistical Procedures Through Empirical Processes

Even without using likelihoods, different statistical procedures use different indexing classes \(\mathcal F\).

  • The empirical distribution function corresponds to the class \[ \mathcal F_{\mathrm{CDF}} = \{f_t:f_t(x)=\mathbf 1\{x\leq t\},\ t\in\mathbb R\}, \]since\[ P_n f_t = P_n\mathbf 1\{x\leq t\} = F_n(t). \]
  • An \(M\)-estimator may instead involve a loss-function class \[ \mathcal F_M = \{\ell_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}, \] where the empirical criterion is maximizing \(P_n\ell_\theta\) over \(\Theta\)
  • A moment- or Z estimator/estimating-equation-based procedure may involve a class of score or moment functions \[ \mathcal F_Z = \{\psi_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}, \] where the empirical estimating equation is typically \(P_n\psi_\theta=0\)

Given a function class \(\mathcal F\) the corresponding empirical process, indexed naturally by \(f \in \mathcal F\), is

\[ \mathbb G_n(f) = \sqrt n(P_n-P)f, \qquad f\in\mathcal F \] Empirical-process theory studies the stochastic behavior of \(P_n\), through the scaled empirical process \(\mathbb G_n\), uniformly over function classes \(\mathcal F\). Thus, the statistical procedure determines the relevant function class, while the complexity and structure of that class determine which empirical-process tools are needed to establish consistency, convergence rates, and asymptotic distributions. This perspective places the asymptotic behavior of many seemingly different statistical procedures and functionals of interest within a single unifying framework.

A Note on Indexing

An empirical process is fundamentally indexed by functions \(f\in\mathcal F\):

\[ \mathbb G_n(f), \qquad f\in\mathcal F \]

When the function class has a natural parameterization \(\mathcal F=\{f_t:t\in T\}\), where \(T\subseteq\mathbb R^d\), we can equivalently index the empirical process by \(t\) and write

\[ \mathbb G_n(t) := \mathbb G_n(f_t) \]

This is only a change in notation: the parameter \(t\) labels the function \(f_t\) on which the empirical process acts.

Example: Indexing Function Classes Differently

  • For the CDF indicator class,\[ f_t(x)=\mathbf 1\{x\leq t\}, \qquad t\in\mathbb R, \]so we can index the empirical process by \(t\) and write \[ \mathbb G_n(t) = \mathbb G_n(f_t) = \sqrt n\{F_n(t)-F(t)\} \]

  • For the class of interval indicators, define \[ f_{a,b}(x) = \mathbf 1\{a<x\leq b\}, \qquad (a,b)\in \left\{ (a,b)\in\mathbb R^2:a<b \right\} \]The empirical process can then be indexed by the vector \((a,b)\),$$\begin{aligned} G_n(a,b) &= G_n(f_{a,b})\ &= n [ {F_n(b)-F_n(a)}

  • {F(b)-F(a)} ]. \end{aligned}$$

  • For a multivariate random variable \(X\in\mathbb R^d\), the multivariate CDF indicator class is \[ f_t(x) = \mathbf 1\{x_1\leq t_1,\ldots,x_d\leq t_d\}, \qquad t=(t_1,\ldots,t_d)\in\mathbb R^d \] Here the empirical process is naturally indexed by \(t\in\mathbb R^d\),\[ \mathbb G_n(t) = \sqrt n\{F_n(t)-F(t)\} \]

  • An \(M\)-estimation problem provides a different type of indexing. For a loss-function class \[ \mathcal G_M = \{\ell_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}, \qquad \Theta\subseteq\mathbb R^p, \] we can index the empirical process by the parameter \(\theta\), \[ \mathbb G_n(\theta) := \mathbb G_n(\ell_\theta) = \sqrt n(P_n-P)\ell_\theta \]

Thus, depending on the structure of the function class, an empirical process may be indexed by a scalar threshold, a vector of thresholds, or a model parameter.

Not every function class has a useful finite-dimensional parameterization. Classes of nuisance functions or other infinite-dimensional objects may still need to be indexed directly by the functions \(f\in\mathcal F\).

Asymptotics and “Well-Behaved” Classes of Functions for EP

The ordinary law of large numbers gives pointwise convergence: for each fixed \(P\)-integrable function \(f\in L_1(P)\),

\[ P_n f\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}Pf \]

We can prove this using Chebyshev’s inequality under the stronger condition \(f\in L_2(P)\), but the ordinary i.i.d. law of large numbers itself requires only the moment condition \(E_P|f(X)|<\infty\).

The classical Glivenko–Cantelli theorem strengthens this pointwise convergence for the CDF indicator class to uniform convergence over all thresholds \(t\in\mathbb R\). Inspired by this result, we call a function class \(\mathcal F\) a \(P\)-Glivenko–Cantelli class if

\[ \sup_{f\in\mathcal F} \left| P_n f-Pf \right| \overset{P}{\longrightarrow}0 \]

Glivenko–Cantelli classes, however, are not enough for every asymptotic question. This property establishes the uniform consistency of the empirical averages \(P_n f\), but it does not describe their scaled stochastic fluctuations or provide their asymptotic distributions.

If \(\mathcal F={f_1}\) contains a single square-integrable function \(f_1\in L_2(P)\), then its variance exists and the ordinary central limit theorem gives

\[ \mathbb G_n(f_1) = \sqrt n(P_n-P)f_1 \rightsquigarrow N(0,\sigma_{f_1}^2), \]

where

\[ \sigma_{f_1}^2 = \operatorname{Var}_P\{f_1(X)\} = P(f_1-Pf_1)^2 \]

More generally, if \(\mathcal F={f_1,\ldots,f_K}\) is a finite class of square-integrable functions, then the multivariate central limit theorem gives

\[ \begin{pmatrix} \mathbb G_n(f_1)\\ \vdots\\ \mathbb G_n(f_K) \end{pmatrix} \rightsquigarrow N_K(0,\Sigma), \]

where

\[ \Sigma_{jk} = P(f_jf_k)-Pf_j\,Pf_k \]

For an infinite class \(\mathcal F\), the same argument gives finite-dimensional convergence: for every fixed finite collection \(f_1,\ldots,f_K\in\mathcal F\), the corresponding vector of empirical-process evaluations converges to a multivariate normal distribution. Finite-dimensional convergence alone, however, does not control the behavior of \(\mathbb G_n(f)\) simultaneously over all \(f\in\mathcal F\).

The space

\[ \ell^\infty(\mathcal F) = \left\{ z:\mathcal F\to\mathbb R: \sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|z(f)|<\infty \right\} \]

is equipped with the supremum norm

\[ \|z\|_{\mathcal F} = \sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|z(f)| \]

Thus, \(\mathbb G_n\) is viewed as the random function

\[ f\mapsto\mathbb G_n(f) \]

on \(\mathcal F\). To establish weak convergence of \(\mathbb G_n\) in \(\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)\), we generally need two ingredients:

  1. finite-dimensional convergence; and
  2. asymptotic tightness of the entire process.

Tightness is commonly established using the \(L_2(P)\) semimetric

\[ \rho_P(f,g) = \left\{ P(f-g)^2 \right\}^{1/2} \]

and by showing that the empirical process is asymptotically equicontinuous:

\[ \lim_{\delta\downarrow0} \limsup_{n\to\infty} P^* \left( \sup_{\rho_P(f,g)<\delta} \left| \mathbb G_n(f)-\mathbb G_n(g) \right| > \varepsilon \right) = 0 \]

for every \(\varepsilon>0\), together with suitable total boundedness of \(\mathcal F\) under \(\rho_P\). In practice, entropy bounds, bracketing numbers, and VC-class results provide sufficient conditions for this tightness.

A function class \(\mathcal F\) is called \(P\)-Donsker if

\[ \mathbb G_n \rightsquigarrow \mathbb G_P \qquad \text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F), \]

where \(\mathbb G_P\) is a tight mean-zero Gaussian process. Its covariance function is

\[ \operatorname{Cov}\{\mathbb G_P(f),\mathbb G_P(g)\} = P(fg)-Pf\,Pg \]

Therefore, square integrability of each \(f\in\mathcal F\) gives the required pointwise and finite-dimensional central limit theorems, but it does not by itself imply that \(\mathcal F\) is \(P\)-Donsker. The Donsker property additionally requires enough control over the size and complexity of the entire function class to obtain asymptotic tightness.

EP for Functionals

Donsker convergence describes the joint asymptotic behavior of the centered and scaled empirical averages over \(\mathcal F\). However, it does not automatically give the asymptotic distribution of every nonlinear functional of the empirical measure. If a statistic can be written as \(T(P_n)\), we still need sufficient regularity of the map \(T\), such as Hadamard differentiability, to transfer the empirical-process convergence through \(T\). The functional delta method then gives

\[ \sqrt n\{T(P_n)-T(P)\} \rightsquigarrow T'_P(\mathbb G_P). \]

Thus, Glivenko–Cantelli classes provide uniform laws of large numbers, Donsker classes provide uniform central limit theorems, and the functional delta method transfers these process-level results to sufficiently regular functionals of interest.

EP Decomposition for Asymptotics

A useful way to organize an asymptotic analysis is to start from the empirical score at the truth and apply the empirical-process decomposition for an estimated score: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb P_n\psi(W;\tau_0,\hat\eta)-\underbrace{P\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0)}_{=0} &= \mathbb P_n\psi(W;\tau_0,\hat\eta)-\textcolor{blue}{\mathbb P_n\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0)} +\textcolor{blue}{\mathbb P_n\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0)}\\ &-\textcolor{red}{P\psi(W;\tau_0,\hat\eta)} +\textcolor{red}{P\psi(W;\tau_0,\hat\eta)} -P\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0) \\[0.5em] &= \underbrace{(\mathbb P_n-P)\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0)}_{\text{first-order empirical fluctuation / influence}}+ \underbrace{(\mathbb P_n-P)\big[\psi(W;\tau_0,\hat\eta)-\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0)\big]}_{\text{empirical-process remainder}}\\&+ \underbrace{P\big[\psi(W;\tau_0,\hat\eta)-\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0)\big]}_{\text{plug-in bias}}. \end{aligned} \]

This decomposition separates the score into three parts:

  1. The first term, \[ (\mathbb P_n-P)\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0), \] is the leading first-order empirical fluctuation term. Since the estimating equation is centered at the truth, \[ P\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0)=0, \] this term is a mean-zero empirical average: \[ (\mathbb P_n-P)\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \psi(W_i;\tau_0,\eta_0). \] It therefore lives on the natural \(n^{-1/2}\) scale and is the term that drives the asymptotic distribution. This is why it is closely related to the influence function: it is the raw first-order empirical process term built from the true score.

    To see how the influence function arises, let \[ \Psi(\tau,\eta):=P\psi(W;\tau,\eta). \] Since \(\hat\tau\) approximately solves the estimating equation, \[ \mathbb P_n\psi(W;\hat\tau,\hat\eta)\approx 0, \] and since the nuisance remainder terms are shown to be negligible, the leading behavior comes from replacing the empirical score near \(\hat\tau\) by its idealized first-order form. A first-order Taylor expansion of the population moment map in \(\tau\) around \(\tau_0\) gives \[ \Psi(\hat\tau,\eta_0) = \Psi(\tau_0,\eta_0) + \dot\Psi_\tau(\tau_0,\eta_0)(\hat\tau-\tau_0) + o(|\hat\tau-\tau_0|), \] where \[ \dot\Psi_\tau(\tau_0,\eta_0) = \frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}P\psi(W;\tau,\eta_0)\bigg|_{\tau=\tau_0}. \] Because \(\Psi(\tau_0,\eta_0)=P\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0)=0\), this becomes \[ \Psi(\hat\tau,\eta_0) \approx \dot\Psi_\tau(\tau_0,\eta_0)(\hat\tau-\tau_0). \] Combining this linearization with the empirical fluctuation term yields \[ 0 \approx (\mathbb P_n-P)\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0) + \dot\Psi_\tau(\tau_0,\eta_0)(\hat\tau-\tau_0), \] so \[ \hat\tau-\tau_0 \approx -\dot\Psi_\tau(\tau_0,\eta_0)^{-1} (\mathbb P_n-P)\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0). \]

    This is exactly where the influence function comes from in the earlier definitions. In the RAL viewpoint, the influence function is the function \(\varphi\) in an expansion of the form \[ \hat\tau-\tau_0 = (\mathbb P_n-P)\varphi + o_p(n^{-1/2}). \] Comparing with the display above, we identify \[ \varphi(W) = -\dot\Psi_\tau(\tau_0,\eta_0)^{-1}\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0). \] In the functional / pathwise viewpoint, this same \(\varphi\) is the first-order derivative kernel that converts a small perturbation of the underlying law into a first-order change in the target. In the Hilbert space / tangent space viewpoint, this same object is the representer of that derivative in \(L_2^0(P)\). So in this centered estimating-equation setting, these earlier influence-function definitions all collapse to the same concrete formula: the score that defines the estimator, after Taylor linearization and normalization by the slope of the population moment map, becomes the influence function.

    So this first term is not itself yet the influence function of the estimator. Rather, it is the centered empirical fluctuation term built from the true score, and after Taylor linearization of the population moment map in \(\tau\), that same score becomes the influence function up to the normalizing slope matrix \(\dot\Psi_\tau(\tau_0,\eta_0)^{-1}\).

  2. The second term, \[ (\mathbb P_n-P)\big[\psi(W;\tau_0,\hat\eta)-\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0)\big], \] is the empirical-process remainder induced by replacing the true nuisance functions with estimated ones. It corresponds to bias from overfitting and sample reuse.

  3. The third term, \[ P\big[\psi(W;\tau_0,\hat\eta)-\psi(W;\tau_0,\eta_0)\big], \] is the population plug-in bias. It measures how the population moment changes when \(\eta_0\) is replaced by \(\hat\eta\), and corresponds to bias from regularization or nuisance estimation.

When you evaluate the estimating equation at the true target \(\tau_0\) but with estimated nuisance \(\hat\eta\), the result differs from the ideal mean-zero empirical fluctuation by two extra error terms: one from the empirical process and one from plug-in bias. DML is built to control those last two terms separately: orthogonalization makes the plug-in bias second order, while sample splitting and cross-fitting control the empirical-process remainder. The first term remains and determines the first-order asymptotics.
